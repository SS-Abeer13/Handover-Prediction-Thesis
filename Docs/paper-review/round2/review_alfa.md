# Review — Reviewer alfa

## Summary

The paper asks whether an LTE handover command can be forecast, one to five seconds ahead, from quantities a handset already holds, and what such a forecast is worth once the evaluation protocol and the measurement timestamps are audited rather than assumed. Four drive-test campaigns on one commercial operator in Dhaka/Gazipur (4 continuous sessions, 10,260 samples at 1 Hz, 957 RRC-decoded handover commands, 8,586 usable rows) supply the data; handover instants come from decoded `RRCConnectionReconfiguration` messages carrying `mobilityControlInfo` rather than vendor counters. The task is posed as discrete-time survival — one model estimates per-bin hazards, horizon probabilities are products of hazards — and evaluated under leave-one-campaign-out against a ladder of three weaker splitting rules, with conformal risk control applied at the level of a driving unit. The headline claim is negative and self-inflicted: in 75.9% of handovers the 1 Hz export row stamped before the command already carries the post-handover serving cell, so lagging every export feature by one row moves the one-second AUPRC of the same model from 0.600 to 0.179 (2.6× a 6.8% prevalence floor, AUROC 0.777), against 0.116 for the deployed A3 rule scored as a predictor; random-row splitting is reported to inflate the same number by 48%.

I want to say first what is genuinely good here, because the strongest reading of this manuscript is strong. The alignment audit is a real and useful finding, and the way it is established — a mechanistic account (Eq. 5.1), a falsification test on boundary-crossing executions, a positive control (NUWiNS downsampled end-of-second vs start-of-second, 93.2% vs 4.1% on the same 4,712 handovers) and a negative control (Raca et al.'s sampled G-NetTrack logs, 29.4%) — is better experimental hygiene than most of the literature it criticises. Reporting that the audit destroyed the project's own best number, and reporting the cumulative-maximum and isotonic controls that weaken the case for the authors' own formulation, is the kind of thing reviewers ask for and rarely get. The per-episode correction to the A3 conversion rate and the handover-weighted reading of the offset table are both right in principle. None of what follows disputes that the study was worth doing. What follows disputes whether this manuscript, as submitted, licenses the numbers it prints.

## Major concerns

**1. The manuscript is not reproducible, and — more seriously — it is not internally consistent, which falsifies its own reproducibility claim.**

*Issue.* Appendix C states that "every table reported in Chapters 4 and 5 is regenerated from the stage outputs rather than transcribed, so that a change in an upstream stage propagates to the report rather than silently disagreeing with it." The manuscript contains at least four silent disagreements of exactly that kind, all on headline quantities. The one-second AUPRC of LightGBM under leave-one-campaign-out (LOCO) is 0.179 in Table 5.3 and Table 5.4 and Appendix Table A.1, 0.160 in Table 5.5 (`lgbm`, LOCO column), and 0.157 in Table 5.6 (`hazard` arm). The one-second ECE of the primary model is 0.012 in Table 5.3, 0.036 in Table 5.6, and 0.024 in the prose of Section 5.4 ("an expected calibration error of 0.024 at the one-second horizon"). The ordering-violation rate of independent classifiers is 40.2% in the abstract, Section 4.2, Section 5.4 prose and Chapter 7, and 29.0% in Table 5.6. The mean and largest violations are 0.025 and 0.521 in prose and 0.020 and 0.377 in the table. Meanwhile Table 2.1 records code release and data release as "Planned", and Appendix C names no repository, no commit, no environment, no library versions, no hyperparameter search ranges and no seed values.

*Where.* Table 5.3, Table 5.4, Table 5.5, Table 5.6, Table A.1, Section 5.4 (¶ beginning "The operational meaning of an expected calibration error of 0.024"), Table 2.1 final two rows, Appendix C.

*Why it matters.* A paper whose stated contribution is "a reproducible demonstration of how much of an apparently strong result on drive-test data can be timestamp alignment" (abstract) is making a procedural claim about how results in this field should be produced. Such a claim is worth nothing without an artifact, and it is worth less than nothing when the manuscript's own regeneration pipeline demonstrably failed to keep four headline numbers in agreement. A reader cannot tell which of 0.157, 0.160 and 0.179 is the result, and therefore cannot tell what any of the comparisons built on it mean.

*What would address it.* Release the audit script, the four protocol implementations and the table-generation pipeline under a DOI before review, not "before submission". Reconcile every duplicated quantity to a single value and state which stage produces it. If the three AUPRC figures differ because the arms differ (e.g. Table 5.6 pools over seeds differently, Table 5.5 re-tunes per protocol), say so in each caption — as printed they are labelled identically.

**2. The "protocol reorders the leaderboard" contribution rests on a table that contradicts the primary results table.**

*Issue.* Contribution 3 and Section 5.3 assert that protocol inflation "is architecture-dependent, so the protocol determines the ranking of models and not only their level," and the concrete evidence is: "Under leave-one-campaign-out the leading learner at one second is logistic regression; under random-row splitting it is LightGBM." That reordering exists only in Table 5.5, which gives LOCO values of `lr` 0.167 and `lgbm` 0.160. Table 5.4 — same protocol, same horizon, same stated conditions ("identical folds, features, formulation and tuning budget") — gives logistic regression 0.130 and LightGBM 0.179, a gap of 0.049 in the opposite direction. Both cannot be true. Further, the quoted inflation figures are computed against the Table 5.5 baseline: 0.236/0.160 = +48% and 0.180/0.160 = +13%. Against the Table 5.3 baseline of 0.179 the same numbers are +32% and +0.6%, and the 13% block-inflation claim — repeated in the abstract, Contribution 3, Section 5.3 and Chapter 7 — evaporates entirely.

*Where.* Table 5.4 vs Table 5.5; Section 5.3 ¶3–4; Contribution 3 (§1.6); abstract; §7.1.

*Why it matters.* This is the contribution the paper says has "the widest implication for the comparator literature," and it is the basis for telling an entire field that its protocol invalidates its rankings. If the LOCO column of Table 5.5 is wrong, the reordering claim disappears. If Table 5.4 is wrong, the primary result disappears. Either way one of the two most-cited tables in the paper is not what it says it is.

*What would address it.* Print one LOCO column, produced by one pipeline, and rebuild Table 5.5 as a delta against it. Attach uncertainty: with four sessions, a 0.007 gap between `lr` and `lgbm` is far inside the per-campaign spread the paper itself reports (Table 5.8: 0.132–0.233 across held-out campaigns), so even on the Table 5.5 numbers the reordering is not distinguishable from fold noise. If the reordering survives, demonstrate it with per-campaign paired values, not pooled point estimates.

**3. The A3 "conversion rate" per episode is arithmetically impossible as a conversion rate.**

*Issue.* Table 5.12 reports, per campaign, the number of A3 trigger episodes "Followed by a command within 2 s": 531, 349, 480 and 287, totalling 1,647. The corresponding campaigns contain 307, 176, 297 and 177 handover commands (Table 3.2), totalling 957. Every stratum has roughly twice as many "converting" episodes as there are commands available to convert into. The per-profile row that carries the headline — the +1 dB profile "declined on only 2.3% of its episodes," 717 of 734 episodes converting — is attributed only 472 handovers in Table 3.1. Episodes are therefore not independent trials on a shared event; multiple concurrent `measId` episodes are being matched to the same command, and the quantity reported is a co-occurrence rate, not a conversion rate.

*Where.* Table 5.12 (all strata), Table 3.2, Table 3.1; Section 5.9 ¶3; Contribution 6; abstract; §7.1.

*Why it matters.* The entire substantive reading — "when this network's mobility trigger fires, a handover almost always follows," and the inference that negative-offset profiles are "neighbour-monitoring rather than mobility triggers" — depends on the 2.3% figure being a decline rate. Under a many-to-one matching, a profile that re-reports on several measurement identities simultaneously will show a spuriously low decline rate precisely because it is verbose, which is the opposite of the paper's interpretation. This also undercuts the paper's own criticism of the per-report unit: the episode unit has a denominator problem of the same family, just smaller.

*What would address it.* Define the matching explicitly (episode → command, one-to-one, nearest unmatched command within 2 s), report how many commands are matched by more than one episode, and recompute. Alternatively invert the unit and report, per handover command, how many episodes preceded it — which is the quantity the current table actually measures.

**4. Leave-one-campaign-out does not close the leakage channel the paper itself identifies as the mechanism.**

*Issue.* Section 3.2 states that handovers "cluster at particular junctions rather than distributing uniformly along the route" and draws the consequence for random splitting: a model "can learn those locations from the training rows and recognise them in the test rows of the same drive." Figure 3.1 places three of the four campaigns on a single OpenStreetMap extent in Dhaka. Section 3.5 confirms the configuration set is stable across all four campaigns. The paper never reports how many physical cell identities, junctions or route kilometres are shared between the held-out campaign and the three training campaigns. A model trained on three overlapping Dhaka corridors and tested on a fourth can recognise the same cells and the same junctions; that is location memorisation across the split boundary, and LOCO does not remove it.

*Where.* §3.2 ¶2; Figure 3.1; §4.9 ("A campaign is one continuous session, which is the only unit in this dataset whose independence can be defended"); Table 5.8.

*Why it matters.* The title is "Leakage-Audited". The paper audits leakage inside a row (alignment) and between rows of a session (splitting unit), and asserts independence at the campaign level rather than testing it. The one campaign that is geographically disjoint — 15 Sept, highway — is also the weakest (AUPRC 0.132 against 0.196–0.233 for the three Dhaka campaigns, Table 5.8), which is exactly the pattern spatial leakage in the other three folds would produce. The paper reads this spread as corridor-and-speed variation; spatial overlap is an equally good explanation and is not excluded.

*What would address it.* Report the Jaccard overlap of serving-cell PCI sets between each held-out campaign and its training set, and the fraction of held-out rows whose serving cell appears in training. Add a spatially blocked arm (hold out a geographic tile across all campaigns) as a fifth rung on the ladder of Figure 4.2. Alternatively, present 15 Sept as the only honest external test and report it separately from the pooled figure, rather than averaging it into a headline.

**5. The external-validation experiment does not validate the model whose results the paper reports, and its conflict of interest is disclosed only partially.**

*Issue.* Section 5.7 rebuilds "both domains ... from the L3 signalling alone, on the same feature construction." But Section 4.6 and Table 4.3 state that the signalling block is "reported as an ablation rather than used in the main arm," and Section 5.8 reports that adding it to the main set *lowers* one-second AUPRC from 0.179 to 0.168. Table 5.9 therefore evaluates a different feature set, on a different row set (7,284 vs 8,586), at a different prevalence (7.9% vs 6.8%), under a different split unit ("leave-one-capture-out"). Objective O4 asks whether "the result generalises"; the experiment as run cannot answer that, because the result was never produced on these features. Separately, the transferred model scores AUROC 0.760 on the public data without refitting, *above* the 0.711 obtained by training on that data and above the authors' own 0.730 — an inversion that normally signals a target domain that is easier or shorter (1,455 rows) and is left entirely unexplained. Finally, reference [38] — the "independently collected public dataset" — lists M. T. Kawser among its authors, who is also the supervisor of this thesis; reference [39], the RL comparator "available on this network," has the same author group. Section 6.6 discloses that the dataset "was produced in the same department as this work," which is true but is not the disclosure a journal requires.

*Where.* §5.7; Table 5.9; §4.6 and Table 4.3; §5.8 final ¶; refs [38], [39]; §6.6 "Ethical and legal".

*Why it matters.* O4 is one of five objectives and is claimed as answered in §7.1. As constructed, the external check tests a feature construction the paper does not use, on data collected by the supervisor's own group with the same instrument, licence, operator and city. That is a same-lab replication, and the AUROC inversion is the kind of anomaly that same-lab data pipelines produce.

*What would address it.* State the supervisor's co-authorship of [38] and [39] in a Conflict of Interest statement, not in an OBE chapter. Run the transfer on the main feature set (accepting that the public release lacks a periodic radio export, in which case say plainly that the primary model cannot be externally validated on available data). Explain the 0.760 > 0.730 > 0.711 ordering, ideally by matching row counts and prevalence between domains. Consider whether [38] is best described as "independent" at all.

**6. The mechanism behind the alignment defect does not close numerically on the paper's own quantities.**

*Issue.* Three statements in Section 5.1 cannot all hold. (a) "65% of commands record a non-zero interruption" — so roughly 35% have x ≈ 0, for which m = 1 − δ − x > 0 always. (b) Over 903 handovers in the last fifth of a second, contamination is "0% when less than 50 ms remain." Under (a), that bin should show contamination of at least ~35% from the zero-interruption handovers alone. (c) The p90 of x is 99 ms and the maximum is 105 ms; under δ ~ Uniform[0,1), the expected fraction of handovers with m < 0 is E[x] ≈ 0.03–0.05, i.e. 30–45 of 938, whereas only 15 are reported. The falsification test — the strongest evidence in the paper for the mechanism — rests on those 15 events.

*Where.* §5.1 ¶ beginning "The prediction is testable on our own captures"; ¶ beginning "The tail of the second then measures"; Figure 5.1(d),(f); Table A.3.

*Why it matters.* The mechanism is what converts an observation into a transferable recommendation, and it is what licenses the further claim that the contamination curve "estimates the serving-cell update delay at roughly 60 ms without any sub-second logging" (Contribution 2). If x has 35% mass at zero, the 60 ms "cliff" is not the update-delay distribution. The supporting cross-check — "consistent with the native ten-hertz view of the same events, where the target is already serving at tau + 100 ms in 100% of handovers" — cannot discriminate 60 ms from 100 ms on a 10 Hz grid, so it confirms nothing about the location of the cliff.

*What would address it.* Report the full distribution of x (including the zero mass and what "non-zero interruption" means operationally — is a zero a true zero or an unlogged `RRCConnectionReconfigurationComplete`?). Show the predicted versus observed count of boundary-crossing handovers. Either reconcile the 0%-below-50 ms bin with the zero-interruption mass, or withdraw the sub-second-delay recovery claim and keep the audit, which stands on its own.

**7. The operating point contradicts the operational story the paper tells around it.**

*Issue.* Chapter 1 motivates the work by asserting that "a warning one second ahead of a handover command is sufficient time for the network to complete target-cell preparation," and Section 2.8 cites Deb et al. [29] for the point that conditional handover "consumes a lead time rather than a label." Table A.2, the only place an operating point appears, reports that at the 5% false-positive point the one-second horizon detects 34.0% of events against a 64.6% instrument ceiling, with 179.0 false alarms per hour (one every 20 seconds) and a *median lead time of 0.47 s*. At 5 s the median lead is 3.31 s with 69.6 false alarms per hour and 42.5% detection. The abstract, Section 5.2 and Chapter 7 report only AUPRC, AUROC and lift, and never state the detection rate, the false-alarm rate or the lead time.

*Where.* Table A.2 (appendix); §1.2; §2.8; §6.6 "Societal"; abstract; §7.1.

*Why it matters.* By the paper's own imported criterion, a 0.47 s median lead does not buy conditional-handover preparation, and 179 false alarms/hour is not an operating point. The paper is scrupulous about not overclaiming on causality and on generalisation, then omits from every summary the single table that says what the warning would actually do. Moving it to an appendix, unmentioned in the abstract, reads as selective emphasis in a paper whose thesis is that the field emphasises selectively.

*What would address it.* Promote Table A.2 into Section 5.2, state the event-level numbers in the abstract next to the AUPRC, and say explicitly whether the paper claims the warning is actionable at these rates. If it is not, the honest framing — which this manuscript is otherwise capable of — is that the task is harder than the literature reports and that the achievable operating points are not yet deployable.

**8. Inference from four units is acknowledged and then repeatedly ignored.**

*Issue.* Section 3.6 and Section 4.9 state correctly that four sessions are too few for a paired test (minimum two-sided sign-test p = 0.125) and that block-bootstrap intervals are "conditional on these four sessions." The paper then reports as findings: a 48% vs 13% inflation contrast with no interval; a per-profile decline contrast of 2.3% vs 49.5%; a Hawkes branching ratio of 0.653 with per-campaign range 0.487–0.675; a learner reordering; and a conformal frontier. Two specific problems compound this. First, Section 5.4 states that "the hazard arm has the lower calibration error on 0 of the four held-out campaigns at one second," yet Table 5.6 gives the hazard arm a *lower* pooled ECE (0.036 vs 0.047) and Section 5.4 concludes it retains "competitive calibration" — a 0-of-4 result with a favourable pooled value needs an explanation, not a favourable gloss. Second, Table 5.7 reports a row at α = 0.05 even though Section 4.10 derives a feasibility floor of α ≥ 1/(n+1) = 0.067 at n = 14, and the surrounding text says only 25% of rotations can express α = 0.05 at all; the table nonetheless prints an 86% alarm rate and a realised miss rate of 0.018 for a target the procedure cannot certify. Third, pooled out-of-fold AUPRC (Table 5.3, Table 5.5) is computed by concatenating scores from four differently-fitted, differently-calibrated models; AUPRC is threshold-free but not scale-free across folds, and no justification for pooling is given.

*Where.* §3.6 ¶3; §4.9 "Repetition and uncertainty"; §5.4 final ¶; Table 5.6; Table 5.7 row α = 0.05; §4.10 Eq. (4.11); Tables 5.3 and 5.5.

*Why it matters.* The paper's rhetorical position is that it is more careful than the field it audits. That position is only defensible if the caution is applied to its own contrasts, not only to its own model scores.

*What would address it.* Attach per-campaign values (not just pooled) to every contrast that carries a claim, including the protocol ladder. Delete or explicitly mark as infeasible the α = 0.05 row. Report fold-wise AUPRC alongside the pooled figure, or pool by averaging fold AUPRCs weighted by fold prevalence and say which convention is used. Reconcile the 0-of-4 calibration result with the pooled ECE.

**9. The novelty and "none of the 22" claims are not auditable, and the closest prior systems are named but never scored.**

*Issue.* The negative-existence claims that carry the paper ("none holds out the mobility unit ... none reports calibration ... none states the alignment") derive from a screen of 108 publications reduced to 22, for which no search strings, databases, date range, inclusion/exclusion criteria, PRISMA flow or second rater are reported. Table D.1 itself contains "NOT VERIFIED" (row 11) and "nan" cells (rows 11, 15, 20, 21), which Table 2.1 nonetheless tallies as non-compliance. Two named comparators are never scored: Prognos, inside [Hassan et al., "Vivisecting mobility management in 5G cellular networks," SIGCOMM 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544216.3544217), is called "the closest prior system to the one built here" and excluded from the audit on the grounds that it is "a system component rather than a published prediction model"; and Section 2.10.1 promises that "Chapter 5 reports a reimplementation of that comparator [the RL study, ref. 39] scored as a predictor, for completeness" — no such row appears in Table 5.4, Table A.1 or anywhere in Chapter 5. Separately, Section 2.5's framing that the survival literature "has not been directed at mobility data" is too strong: hazard and sojourn-time modelling of cell residence is long established in the cellular analytical literature, e.g. [Towards Understanding the Fundamentals of Mobility in Cellular Networks](https://arxiv.org/html/1204.3447v2) and [Handover Rate and Sojourn Time Analysis in Mobile Heterogeneous Networks](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.05019); neither is cited. The correct narrow claim is *learned, covariate-conditional discrete-time hazards fitted to measured signalling*, not time-to-event modelling of mobility as such. The related point holds for the alignment contribution: temporal misalignment and window-aggregation leakage are recognised failure modes with a general methodological literature, e.g. [Auditing Data Leakage and Temporal Misalignment in Machine-Learning Prognosis of Breast Cancer](https://doi.org/10.3390/biomedinformatics6040060) and Kaufman et al. [36], which the paper cites but does not use to position the contribution as a domain instantiation rather than a new phenomenon.

*Where.* §1.2 ¶3; §2.3; Table 2.1; Appendix D Table D.1; §2.10.1 final ¶; §2.5 ¶1; §7.4.

*Why it matters.* "None of 22" is the load-bearing justification for every contribution in Section 1.6, and it is asserted on a screen whose reproducibility is exactly what the paper demands of others. Scoring Prognos and the RL comparator matters more than the audit: they are the two systems an operator would compare against, and a 2.6× lift over prevalence is uninterpretable without them.

*What would address it.* Publish the screening protocol and the 108-record list. Recode "NOT VERIFIED"/"nan" as a separate category and report Table 2.1 with and without them. Either deliver the promised comparator reimplementation or delete the sentence promising it. Soften §2.5 and cite the analytical sojourn-time literature, and position the alignment audit as the first drive-test instantiation of a known leakage class rather than as a new class.

**10. Missing sections and a broken citation apparatus make parts of the argument uncheckable.**

*Issue.* Sections 5.12 ("Discussion against the objectives") and 5.13 ("Threats to validity") appear in the table of contents and are cross-referenced in the body — §5.10 defers a causal claim "for the reason given in Section 5.13" — but neither exists in the manuscript, which runs from Table 5.15 directly into a reference list. Sections 6.1–6.4 and 7.3 are likewise absent. The manuscript carries two complete, mutually inconsistent reference lists: in one [18] is Candès, Lei and Ren, "Conformalized survival analysis"; in the other [18] is Cohen, Park, Simeone and Shamai, "Calibrating AI models for wireless communications" — and Sections 2.5 and 2.6, four paragraphs apart, cite "[18]" for each. [21] is Angelopoulos et al. (conformal risk control) in the first list and Hawkes in the second, and both are cited as [21] (§1.6 Contribution 5, §4.10 Eq. 4.11 vs §2.7, §4.11). [30] is Wagner et al. in §4.9 and LightGBM in §4.7. Reference [3]'s author initials differ between lists. References [6], [15], [38] and [39] carry editorial notes to the authors ("[Author list to be completed from the ACM record before submission]", "confirm the version DOI before submission", "[Venue to be confirmed]"). An orphan paragraph ("Instrument ceiling. The one-hertz export caps event-level detection at 64.6% ...") sits stranded inside the reference list.

*Where.* Table of contents vs body (§5.12, §5.13, §6.1–6.4, §7.3); both reference lists; §2.5, §2.6, §2.7, §4.7, §4.9, §4.10, §1.6.

*Why it matters.* The threats-to-validity section is the one a reviewer most needs in a paper making this many bounded claims, and it is missing while being cited. Attribution to Candès et al. versus Cohen et al. is not cosmetic: Section 2.5 uses [18] specifically to disclaim novelty for conformalised survival analysis, and the reader cannot tell which paper is being deferred to.

*What would address it.* Supply the missing sections; collapse to one reference list; renumber; remove every editorial placeholder; verify each in-text citation against the final list. Strip Chapter 6 entirely for a journal submission (see minor concerns).

## Minor concerns

- Feature counts do not reconcile. §2.10 Stage 3 says 152 candidate features; §4.6 says "three blocks and 112 columns"; Table 4.3 says "Main feature set | 112" but its blocks sum to 106 (83 + 17 + 6); §4.7 says logistic regression runs "over the 107 features"; Appendix B says 107 retained and lists blocks summing to 120 (83 + 17 + 13 + 7). The signalling block is 10 in Table 4.3 and 13 in Appendix B; the history block is 6 and 7 respectively.
- Ping-pong endpoints differ in three places on two denominators: §2.4 says "24.5% to 41.3%" on 938 handovers; Table 5.13 and §5.10 say 26.3% to 43.8% on 957; §7.1 says "26.3% to 41.3%" on 938. Also §3.4 states the 957 count is used "in exactly one place: the ungrouped variant of the ping-pong definition ladder in Section 5.9," but ping-pong is §5.10 and Table 5.13 uses 957 for all four rows.
- Table 5.14 does not reconcile with Table 5.13. The carrier split (748 × 27.1% + 209 × 7.2%)/957 = 22.7%, against the 26.3% headline; the regime split reconciles to 26.3% exactly. One of the two strata is computed under a different definition.
- Table 3.1 does not close. The six A3 profile rows sum to 648 handovers, not the "651 A3 total" stated in the final row; all eleven rows sum to 907 of 957 commands (94.8%), leaving 50 unaccounted for and unexplained. 484/651 = 74.4%, not the stated 74.8%.
- The branching ratio is misread. §5.10 says a branching ratio of 0.653 means "each handover command is followed on average by roughly 0.7 further commands attributable to it" — 0.653 is expected *direct* offspring; expected total offspring is n/(1−n) ≈ 1.9. Moreover §4.11 and §5.10 report that the Ogata residual test rejects the exponential kernel, so a branching ratio estimated under that kernel should not be interpreted quantitatively, yet it is.
- Section 4.7 contradicts itself within four paragraphs: the sequence models "consume windows of the ten most recent feature vectors — the same vectors, lagged the same way," then "each consuming a window of raw per-second measurements rather than the engineered summary features."
- The 0.5 s horizon is largely unresolvable by the instrument. Table A.2 reports 34.2% of events resolvable on the 1 Hz grid at 0.5 s, yet 0.5 s is carried as a headline horizon in O1, Table 4.1, Table 4.2, Table 5.3 and Table A.1 without that caveat attached.
- The 2-second post-handover blank (§3.4) removes precisely the rows on which a ping-pong warning would have to be issued, while §1.2 and §6.6 motivate the work partly by the possibility of suppressing a reversal. This tension is never addressed.
- Conformal calibration-set size is stated three ways: "28 held-out calibration drives" (§5.5 prose), "the 14 calibration blocks available in a held-out campaign" (§4.10), and "8 to 20 blocks per campaign" (§5.5 ¶3).
- Equation numbering skips (4.10); Figure 4.1's caption line is duplicated verbatim; §5.4 points to "Figure 5.8" for the coherence axes when the list of figures assigns that to the burst analysis (should be 5.7); the list of tables contains two independent Table 5.5–5.12 series.
- Distance driven is 78 km in §3.6 and "approximately 95 km" in Table 6.6.
- The abstract juxtaposes "AUPRC 0.475 at five seconds (1.8x), against 0.116 for the deployed A3 rule scored as a predictor" — 0.116 is the A3 one-second figure; at five seconds A3 reaches 0.374 (Table 5.4).
- Operator anonymity is nominal: the city, department, instrument licence and the dataset DOI of [38] together identify the network. If anonymity is a stated ethical commitment (§6.6), it should be either enforced or dropped.
- Table D.1 contains several entries with arXiv identifiers implying 2026 postings (e.g. arXiv:2603.17158, arXiv:2605.12453, arXiv:2512.11841) and years ahead of the stated campaign dates; these should be verified against the records.
- Chapter 6 (outcome-based education mapping, CO/PO/K/P/A tables, project budget) is a degree-programme requirement with no place in a journal submission and should be removed along with the declaration, approval and acknowledgement front matter.
- §1.5 and §7.2 both state that "only a counting upper bound is reported" for the benefit of acting on the warning; no such counting bound appears anywhere in Chapter 5.

## Verdict

**Major revision.** The measurement work, the alignment audit and the willingness to report results that damage the authors' own earlier claims are genuinely above the standard of the surrounding literature and are worth publishing — but as submitted the manuscript prints three different values for its headline AUPRC, three for its headline ECE and two for its headline violation rate, builds its protocol contribution on a table that contradicts its primary results table, reports a conversion rate whose numerator exceeds the events available to it, and promises code, data and two cross-referenced sections that do not exist.
