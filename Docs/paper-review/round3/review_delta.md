# Review — Reviewer delta

## Summary

The paper asks whether an LTE handover command can be forecast one to five seconds ahead from handset-observable measurements, using four drive-test campaigns (4 continuous sessions, 10,260 one-hertz samples, 957 RRC-decoded handover commands) on one commercial operator in Dhaka/Gazipur. Its methodological core is an alignment audit showing that the one-hertz XCAL export row stamped at *t* describes the state at approximately *t+1*, so that in 75.9% of handovers the pre-command row already carries the post-handover serving cell; lagging every export feature one row drops one-second AUPRC from 0.600 to 0.179. The task is then posed as discrete-time survival (hazards multiplied into horizon probabilities, so ordering holds by construction), evaluated leave-one-campaign-out, with a conformal risk-control threshold whose exchangeable unit is a driving unit. Headline claims: AUPRC 0.179 at one second against a 6.8% floor (2.6× lift, AUROC 0.777) versus 0.116 for the deployed A3 rule; random-row splitting inflates the same number by 48%; independent per-horizon classifiers violate horizon ordering on 40.2% of rows; and two corrected network characterisations (A3 conversion rate has no unit-free value; 75% of A3-triggered handovers fire under a positive offset).

## Major concerns

**1. The novelty claim for the survival formulation rests on a literature screen that excludes the literature that would contradict it.**

*Issue.* Section 2.5 asserts that survival analysis "supplies exactly the right mathematical objects — a discrete-time hazard … — but [has] not been directed at mobility data"; Section 2.9 restates this as a gap ("The correct formulation exists in the survival literature, but has not been applied to mobility events"); Contribution 4 and Table 6.5 attribute innovation to "the application of a discrete-time hazard formulation … to mobility prediction." This is false as stated. There is a substantial, mature body of work modelling exactly the time-to-next-handover distribution for a mobile UE — cell sojourn time and handover rate analysis — none of which appears anywhere in the paper. See J.-S. Shin et al., "Analysis of Cell Sojourn Time in Heterogeneous Networks With Small Cells," IEEE (https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7377029); M. Salehi and E. Hossain, "Handover Rate and Sojourn Time Analysis in Mobile Drone-Assisted Cellular Networks" (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.05019); and "Stochastic Geometry Analysis of Sojourn Time in Multi-Tier Cellular Networks" (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.01884). That literature derives the distribution of *T*, i.e. the survival function this paper estimates, for mobility events in cellular networks. It is analytical rather than learned and conditions on geometry rather than on covariates, which is a real and statable difference — but the paper does not state it, because it does not acknowledge the literature exists. The same omission affects Section 2.4's treatment of the pre-2020 mobility-prediction-for-handover line (e.g. https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/7248939), which the screen appears not to have reached at all: Appendix D contains no entry earlier than 2020 and is dominated by 2024–2026 arXiv preprints.

*Where.* Sections 2.5, 2.9; Contribution 4 (Section 1.6); Table 6.3 (K8), Table 6.5 (A3); Appendix D.

*Why it matters.* The paper's entire rhetorical structure is "the field omits the unglamorous things, and we supply them." That structure is only credible if the screen is exhaustive and adversarially constructed. A screen that returns zero pre-2020 work and misses the analytical time-to-handover literature is not a gap analysis; it is a gap constructed by the choice of search frame. Because the gap statement is what licenses the paper's contributions, a reader cannot presently tell which of the six contributions are genuinely unoccupied.

*What would address it.* State the screening protocol reproducibly — databases, query strings, date range, inclusion/exclusion criteria, PRISMA-style counts from 108 to 22 — and re-run it with queries covering "sojourn time", "cell residence time", "handover rate", "time-to-event" and "survival". Then restate the claim in the only form the evidence can support: the discrete-time hazard has been used to obtain *conditional, covariate-driven, per-sample* multi-horizon probabilities on *measured* mobility data, in contrast to the analytical marginal distributions of the stochastic-geometry literature. That is a defensible claim; the current one is not.

**2. The single closest prior system is removed from the audit by a definitional move, and the audit's own counts are demonstrably wrong.**

*Issue.* Prognos (inside Hassan et al. [5], SIGCOMM 2022) is described as "the closest prior system to the one built here" and "the strongest system in the neighbourhood of this work" — and is then excluded from the 22 audited models on the grounds that it is "a system component rather than a published prediction model with its own protocol" (Section 2.3). That exclusion is convenient rather than principled: every "0/22" and "2/22" row in Table 2.1 is used later as evidence that *nobody* does X, and the one system most likely to falsify some of those rows is the one carved out. The carve-out is also factually weak — the SIGCOMM'22 work ships a public artifact (https://github.com/SIGCOMM22-5GMobility/artifact; paper at https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544216.3544217), so its protocol is auditable in exactly the way the paper says it is not. Separately, Table 2.1's counts are contradicted by the paper's own Appendix D: entry #9 (Amirova et al., *Future Internet* 2026) is listed as REAL-MEASURED, device-level hold-out, with a PR curve and a bootstrap CI on AUC. That is a mobility-unit hold-out on measured data with a prevalence-aware metric, which contradicts "Splits by drive, session or route: 0/22" and sits awkwardly with "Reports calibration: 0/22". Section 2.3's dismissal — "the two most recent additions are regression tasks in which prevalence does not arise" — does not apply to #9, which is not a regression task by the appendix's own reckoning.

*Where.* Section 2.3, Table 2.1, Appendix D (Table D.1, rows 9, 12, 13, 22), Section 1.2.

*Why it matters.* The "0/22 … none … none … none" sentence in Section 2.3 is quoted verbatim in the abstract, Section 1.2, Section 5.4 and Section 7.4. If even one of those rows is wrong, the framing of the whole paper is overstated, and a reader can check row 9 against the paper's own appendix in thirty seconds.

*What would address it.* Audit Prognos on the same rubric and put it in Table 2.1 as a row, not a footnote; if its lead-time result (~900 ms) is comparable, report the comparison numerically rather than asserting that it "neither [reports] calibration nor an alignment statement." Re-check every 0/N and 2/N cell against Appendix D and reconcile the two, or drop the absolute quantifiers in favour of "we found no instance of X among the 22, subject to the screening protocol in Appendix D."

**3. The headline AUPRC has three different values in three tables, and the two tables disagree about which learner wins.**

*Issue.* For the same estimand — LightGBM, hazard formulation, leave-one-campaign-out, one-second horizon, out of fold — the paper reports 0.179 (Table 5.3, Table 5.4, Table A.1, abstract, Section 5.2, Section 5.12, Section 7.1), 0.160 (Table 5.5, LOCO column) and 0.157 (Table 5.6, hazard row). Worse, the learner ordering flips between Table 5.4 and Table 5.5's LOCO column with no explanation: Table 5.4 has LightGBM 0.179 first and logistic regression 0.130 tied for fifth; Table 5.5 has logistic regression 0.167 first and LightGBM 0.160 third. Section 5.3's text ("Under leave-one-campaign-out the leading learner at one second is logistic regression") therefore flatly contradicts Table 5.4 and Figure 5.3, both of which are presented as the primary result.

*Where.* Table 5.3, Table 5.4, Table 5.5, Table 5.6, Table A.1, Sections 5.2, 5.3, 5.12, 7.1, abstract.

*Why it matters.* This is not a rounding difference; it is the number in the abstract. And the ranking reversal is precisely Contribution 3's payload — "the protocol determines the ranking of models and not only their level" — which is established by comparing the LOCO ranking to the random-row ranking. If the LOCO ranking in Table 5.5 is wrong, the reversal claim collapses; if the LOCO ranking in Table 5.4 is wrong, the primary result collapses. Appendix C asserts that "every table reported in Chapters 4 and 5 is regenerated from the stage outputs rather than transcribed, so that a change in an upstream stage propagates to the report rather than silently disagreeing with it." Three tables silently disagree, so that claim is also falsified.

*What would address it.* Identify which pipeline stage produced each table, regenerate all of them from one run, and state explicitly whether the 0.179/0.160/0.157 spread reflects seed averaging, a different calibration arm, or a stale table. If the ranking reversal does not survive, retract Contribution 3's second half and keep only the level inflation.

**4. Expected calibration error at one second is quoted as 0.012, 0.024 and 0.036 in three places, and the formulation loses the per-campaign comparison 0–4 while winning pooled.**

*Issue.* Table 5.3 gives ECE 0.012 at one second; Section 5.2 repeats it. Table 5.6 gives 0.036 for the hazard arm at one second, and Section 5.4 builds its central argument on that value ("0.036 at one second against 0.047 for raw independent heads"). Section 5.4 then states, in the paragraph beginning "The operational meaning," a third value: "an expected calibration error of 0.024 at the one-second horizon." None of the three is reconciled and 0.024 appears nowhere else in the paper. Compounding this, Section 5.4 reports that "the hazard arm has the lower calibration error on 0 of the four held-out campaigns at one second" — while Table 5.6's pooled figure has the hazard arm winning. Winning pooled and losing 0–4 per unit is a textbook aggregation artefact, and it is the *only* comparison the paper's four units can support.

*Where.* Table 5.3, Table 5.6, Section 5.4 (three separate paragraphs), Section 7.1, Contribution 4.

*Why it matters.* After the authors correctly concede that cumulative-max and PAV projections reach zero violations at no cost, the entire remaining case for the hazard formulation is "coherence *and competitive calibration* from a single fit." That case is built on a number that (a) contradicts the primary results table by a factor of three and (b) is reversed on every one of the four independent units. Section 5.4's operational gloss — "among the samples on which the system asserts a probability near 0.8, a handover follows within one second on approximately 80% of occasions" — is also unsupported by anything shown: at a 6.8% prevalence with AUPRC 0.179, it is not established that any rows receive scores near 0.8, and no reliability diagram bins or counts are reported.

*What would address it.* Report one ECE per (arm, horizon, protocol) cell, with the binning scheme and per-bin counts; report the per-campaign ECE table for all arms (promised in Appendix A but absent from it); and either drop the calibration claim or restate it as "pooled ECE favours the hazard arm, but the per-campaign comparison does not, and with four units we cannot resolve the two."

**5. The mechanism section's denominators do not exist, and the falsification test is conditioned on a variable observed for only 65% of events.**

*Issue.* Section 5.1 states: "Over 903 handovers arriving in the last fifth of a second, contamination is 98.2% when 75 to 100 ms remain…". The paragraph sits inside a discussion of "our export," which contains 938 handovers in total; the last fifth of a second cannot contain 903 of them. (903 is approximately one fifth of the NUWiNS corpus's 4,712, so the sentence has almost certainly silently switched datasets mid-paragraph.) Second, the falsification test — "Of the 15 handovers whose execution crosses the second boundary … not one has the target cell on the row stamped *t*" — requires *m = 1 − δ − x*, hence *x*, per handover; but the same paragraph states that only "65% of commands record a non-zero interruption." How *m* was computed for the other 35%, and whether membership in the 15 is confounded with *x* being recorded at all, is not stated. With max *x* = 105 ms, *m* < 0 requires δ > 0.895, which should select roughly 10.5% of 938 ≈ 98 handovers, not 15 — an order-of-magnitude discrepancy that is never addressed. Third, the "control" dataset is not clean by the paper's own standard: Raca et al. show 29.4% of row-*t* samples carrying the new cell, against a stated background of ~12% in the authors' own data, and the paper calls this "there is none."

*Where.* Section 5.1, paragraphs 5–8; Figure 5.1(d)(e)(f); Table A.3.

*Why it matters.* The alignment audit is the paper's strongest and most transferable contribution, and the NUWiNS start-of-second / end-of-second manipulation is genuinely decisive. That makes it all the more damaging that the supporting mechanism analysis contains an impossible denominator, an unexplained selection rule and a control that is not obviously controlled. A referee who catches the 903 will doubt the 15, and then the 60 ms.

*What would address it.* Label every *n* in Section 5.1 with its dataset. Report the joint distribution of δ and *x* for our captures, state how *m* is computed when *x* is missing, and report the count of handovers with δ > 0.895 alongside the 15. For Raca, give the row-*t−1* and row-*t−3* figures so that 29.4% can be read against its own background rather than against ours.

**6. The ground truth is threatened by a re-establishment rate the paper does not confront.**

*Issue.* Table 3.2 records 341 RRC re-establishments against 957 handover commands in 2.9 hours — one re-establishment per 2.8 handover commands, or one every 31 seconds of driving — of which 321 carry `reconfigurationFailure`. Section 3.6 disposes of this in two sentences ("predominantly failures to apply a reconfiguration rather than radio link failures during mobility") and treats them "as a competing event in Section 5.8." Section 5.8 then censors "the 93 rows whose next event is a re-establishment," a figure irreconcilable with 341 events. The distribution across campaigns is extreme (159 in 60 minutes on 13 Sept; 5 in 42 minutes on the highway) and unremarked.

*Where.* Section 1.2, Section 3.6, Table 3.2, Section 5.8.

*Why it matters.* A 33% reconfiguration-failure rate on a live commercial network is not an ordinary operating condition; it is more consistent with an instrumentation, decoding or UE-state problem. If a material fraction of the 957 `RRCConnectionReconfiguration` messages with `mobilityControlInfo` were never successfully applied, then the label "handover command issued at τ" is not the label "handover occurred," and the entire event set — on which every AUPRC, every ping-pong rate and every conversion rate rests — is of uncertain composition. The paper's own alignment mechanism depends on the handover *completing* within the second, which failed reconfigurations by definition do not.

*What would address it.* Report, for each of the 957 commands, whether a matching `RRCConnectionReconfigurationComplete` was decoded and whether a re-establishment followed within a stated window; give the analysis with and without commands that did not complete; and explain the 93-vs-341 discrepancy or withdraw the competing-risk sensitivity.

**7. The A3-conversion section contains arithmetic that cannot be right, and a denominator that is smaller than its own numerator.**

*Issue.* Contribution 6 states that "the loose episode rule lets 2,361 episodes claim 1,647 of 957 commands" — a fraction whose numerator exceeds its denominator. Table 5.12's "command multiplicity" rows report "×650.00 inflation" and "×927.00 inflation"; the correct ratios are 1,647/635 = 2.59 and 4,391/908 = 4.84, so both cells are arithmetic nonsense that survived into a submitted table. Section 5.9 says 635 episodes own a command but also that "651 of 957 handover commands are attributed to an A3 report"; under a one-to-one matching those two numbers must agree, and the 16-event gap is unexplained. Table 3.1 is worse: its rows sum to 907 of 957 commands (50 unaccounted) and its A3 rows sum to 648, not the "651 A3 total" stated in the same table's last row. The 75% headline is therefore 484/651 = 74.3% or 484/648 = 74.7%, neither of which is the stated 74.8%. Section 3.5 and Section 2.2 then mis-attribute the whole 75% to the +1 dB profile alone, which is 472/651 = 72.5%. Finally, Table 5.12's per-profile owned counts (463 for +1 dB; 71+19 = 90 for −10 dB/Hys 2/TTT 640) contradict Table 3.1's attributions for the same profiles (472; 108).

*Where.* Contribution 6 (Section 1.6), Table 3.1, Sections 2.2 and 3.5, Table 5.12, Section 5.9, abstract, Section 7.1.

*Why it matters.* Section 5.9's thesis is that a rate quoted without its unit is "a statement about bookkeeping and not about the network." The section demonstrating that thesis contains four bookkeeping errors, including two in the abstract's own numbers. This is the most self-undermining defect in the paper.

*What would address it.* Rebuild Table 3.1 so that its rows sum to a stated total and its subtotal matches the A3 total used everywhere else; fix the two "inflation" cells; reconcile 635 vs 651; restate the 75% with its exact numerator and denominator and correct the two places that attribute it to a single profile; and rewrite "1,647 of 957" as the multiplicity statement it is meant to be.

**8. Ping-pong is reported on two different event sets with two different ranges, and the two stratifications of Table 5.14 do not give the same total.**

*Issue.* Section 2.4 promises "the full ladder of these choices on a single fixed set of **938** handovers, where the rate moves from **24.5%** to **41.3%**." Section 5.10 and Table 5.13 deliver a ladder on **957** commands moving from **26.3%** to **43.8%**, and Section 5.10 then quotes a third range, "between 21.0% and 43.8%," for the nine-cell grid. Section 3.4 states that "the event-level analyses of Sections 5.9 and 5.10 use all 957 commands" and, two sentences later, that "the 957 figure is used only where the raw event stream is the appropriate object, which in this thesis occurs in exactly one place: the ungrouped variant of the ping-pong definition ladder in **Section 5.9**" — wrong section number, and contradicted by three of Table 5.13's four rows using 957. Substantively: Table 5.14's carrier strata (748 intra at 27.1%, 209 inter at 7.2%) imply 218 ping-pongs = 22.8% of 957, while its regime strata (177 highway at 32.2%, 780 urban at 25.0%) imply 252 = 26.3%. The same fixed command set yields two different totals depending on how it is cut.

*Where.* Section 2.4, Section 3.4, Section 5.10, Tables 5.13 and 5.14.

*Why it matters.* Contribution 6 and the environmental-impact argument of Section 6.6 both rest on the 26.3% figure. If one stratification of the same commands gives 22.8%, either a stratum is incomplete or the definitions differ across the two tables — and a paper whose stated finding is that ping-pong rates are unstable under undeclared definitional choices has just demonstrated that instability in its own two tables.

*What would address it.* Fix the Section 2.4 preview to match Chapter 5. State the definition used for Table 5.14 explicitly and show that the strata partition the 957 commands and recover the headline rate.

**9. Reproducibility: the citation apparatus is broken, the feature count takes five values, and the artifacts are "Planned."**

*Issue.* From Section 2.6 onward the in-text citation numbers are systematically off. Section 2.6 attributes "Calibrating AI models for wireless communications" to [18], which is Candès–Lei–Ren *Conformalized survival analysis* — a paper Section 2.5 correctly cites as [18] one page earlier, so [18] denotes two different works in adjacent sections. Section 2.7 cites "Hawkes [21]" (= Angelopoulos et al., *Conformal risk control*), "Laub et al. [22]" (= Bates et al.), "Ogata [23]" (= Hawkes), "Price-Williams and Heard [24]" (= Laub et al.). Section 2.8 cites "Deep CORAL [26]" (= Price-Williams and Heard) and "Wagner et al. [28]" (= Sun and Saenko), while Section 4.9 cites the same Wagner paper as [30], which is correct. Section 4.10 attributes conformal risk control to [20] and Eq. (4.11) to [21] within two pages. Reference [36] (Kaufman et al. on leakage) is never cited in the text, in a paper about leakage. Feature counts: "152 candidate" (Section 2.10), "112 columns" (Section 4.6), Table 4.3 rows summing to 106 for the main set, "107 features" (Sections 4.7 and Appendix B), Appendix B blocks summing to 120 with History 7 and Signalling 13 against Table 4.3's 6 and 10. Seeds: "five seeds" (Section 2.10 Stage 5) versus "three seeds" (Section 4.9, Table 5.6, Figure 5.19). Driving distance: 78 km (Section 3.6) versus ~95 km (Table 6.6) — and the per-kilometre false-alarm figures in Table A.2 imply ~33 km/h over 2.9 h, i.e. ~96 km, so the 78 km figure is the wrong one. Table 2.1 simultaneously claims "Yes, with the code released" and "Releases code: Planned."

*Where.* Chapter 2 throughout, Section 4.6–4.7, Table 4.3, Appendix B, Appendix C, Table 2.1, Table 6.6.

*Why it matters.* A paper whose closing sentence is that the field omits the least novel and most important things — protocol, alignment, release — cannot arrive with unreleased code, unresolved author lists ([6], [15]), an unconfirmed dataset DOI ([38]), a missing equation number (4.10), and a bibliography whose numbering is off by two for a quarter of the paper. These are the defects the paper exists to criticise.

*What would address it.* Regenerate the bibliography; fix the feature accounting to a single number with a table that sums to it; state the seed count once; correct the distance; and release the audit script, the four protocol implementations and the table-generation pipeline at submission, not "planned." Given the paper's thesis, artifact release should be a condition of acceptance.

**10. The "external validation" is not external, and does not evaluate the primary model.**

*Issue.* Objective O4 promises generalisation "to an independently collected public dataset," and Section 5.12 reports it as met at "AUROC 0.760 on an independent collection." Section 5.7 then discloses that the dataset [38] was collected by a different group *in the same department, under the same supervisor, on the same operator, in the same city, with the same XCAL licence*, and that reference [39] — the comparator method — is by the same authors. The disclosure is commendable and the revised wording ("tests collection, route and time — not operator") is honest. But O4, Section 5.12 and the abstract still trade on the stronger framing. Two further problems: (a) because the public release contains measurement-report exports rather than a periodic radio export, *both* arms of Table 5.9 are rebuilt from L3 signalling alone — so what transfers is a signalling-only model that is **not** the primary model of Table 5.3, and the radio and mobility blocks (100 of 112 features) are never externally tested at all; (b) the transferred model (AUROC 0.760) beats the model trained on the target data itself (0.711) and beats the source in-domain result (0.730), which is an anomaly warranting investigation rather than a headline. Separately, the comparator reimplementation promised in Section 2.10.1 and located by Section 5.13 "in Section 5.11" is absent from Section 5.11 and from Table 5.15 — the only quantitative comparison against the nearest prior work on this network is missing from the paper.

*Where.* Objective O4 (Section 1.4), Section 5.7, Table 5.9, Sections 5.12, 5.13, 6.6, 2.10.1, Table 5.15.

*Why it matters.* The paper's external-validity evidence is one held-out corridor within the same four days and one same-department, same-operator collection evaluated with a different feature set. That supports "the formulation transfers within this operator," which the paper sometimes says and sometimes does not. Given the conflict of interest, the missing comparator result is the one number a sceptical reader most wants.

*What would address it.* Restate O4 to match the evidence. Report the primary (radio + mobility + history) model on the public dataset if any periodic export exists there, or state plainly that it cannot be tested. Investigate and explain the 0.760 > 0.711 anomaly (target-set size, prevalence, file heterogeneity). Add the reimplemented [39] comparator to Table 5.15 with its numbers, or remove the claim that it was scored.

**11. The certified operating point is not an operating point, and the α = 0.05 row reports rotations that cannot express α = 0.05.**

*Issue.* Table A.2 shows the primary model at its "5% false-positive operating point" detecting 34.0% of one-second events at **179 false alarms per hour** — one spurious warning every 20 seconds, on a route where a real handover occurs every 11 seconds. At five seconds it is 69.6/h for 42.5% detection. Meanwhile Table 5.7's conformal frontier reaches α = 0.05 only at an 86% alarm rate, which Section 5.5 itself calls "not an operating point any operator would deploy." Yet Objective O3 ("make the resulting probabilities usable by an operator") is declared "met on both parts" in Section 5.12, and Section 6.6 argues societal benefit from a warning that would fire three times per genuine event. Additionally, Table 5.7 reports "across campaigns" results at α = 0.05 with 100% of rotations respecting the bound, while Section 5.5 states that "only 25% of the rotations can express α = 0.05 at all" — so nine of the twelve rotations contributed something to a row they cannot express, and what they contributed is not stated.

*Where.* Table A.2, Table 5.7, Section 5.5, Section 5.12 (O3), Section 6.6.

*Why it matters.* "Usable by an operator" is an operational claim and the paper supplies the numbers that refute it, then does not reconcile them. An alarm rate that exceeds the event rate makes target-cell preparation a net cost, which bears directly on the motivation in Sections 1.2 and 6.6.

*What would address it.* Report the alarm-rate/detection frontier and identify, explicitly, whether any point on it is operationally sensible given a stated preparation cost; if none is, say so and reframe O3 as calibration-and-guarantee rather than usability. For Table 5.7, restrict each α row to the rotations that can express it and report the count.

**12. The protocol-ladder experiment is not the controlled substitution it claims to be, and the training-set-size confound is unaddressed.**

*Issue.* Section 5.3 states that "everything is held fixed — the data, the lagged features, the formulation, the **hyper-parameters**, the seeds — and only the splitting rule changes." Section 4.7 states that "every arm receives … twenty random-search trials per outer fold, scored on a calibration block held out of that fold's training data." These cannot both be true: if the search is nested inside the evaluation, each protocol tunes a different model, and the 48% "inflation" confounds optimism from near-duplicate rows with a different hyper-parameter selection under a different (and easier) inner validation signal. Nor is training-set size held constant: leave-one-campaign-out trains on three of four campaigns (57–75% of rows depending on the fold), while random-row and random-block splits presumably train on a fixed fraction that is never stated. The A3-rule "control" at +0% across the ladder is offered as evidence that the effect is over-fitting rather than test-set difficulty — but since every protocol scores the same pooled out-of-fold rows, test-set difficulty was constant by construction and the control is vacuous.

*Where.* Section 5.3, Section 4.7, Table 5.5.

*Why it matters.* This is Contribution 3, and Section 5.12 elevates it above every modelling result ("Objective O2 is met, and the answer is larger than the modelling differences the thesis reports"). A 48% effect attributable in unknown proportion to leakage, tuning and training-set size is not a measurement of "what the unit of splitting is worth."

*What would address it.* Re-run the ladder with hyper-parameters frozen at a single configuration and training-set size matched across protocols; report that alongside the nested-search version, and decompose the gap. Also note that "blocking with a purge sits within 4% of LOCO" holds only for LightGBM — TCN (−8.5%), GRU (−11%) and Transformer (+15%) do not — so that recommendation is overgeneralised from one arm.

## Minor concerns

- Two conflicting lists of tables appear in the front matter: lines numbering Tables 5.1–5.15 and a second block numbering Tables 5.5–5.12 with different captions (e.g. "Table 5.9 Event A3 report-conversion rate by campaign … the fraction of A3 reports not followed by a handover command within two seconds").
- The 90.5% "in-principle resolvable" ceiling (Section 1.5) appears in no table; Table A.2 gives 64.6% at one second and 95.3% at five. Its derivation is not given.
- Table A.2's event counts vary by horizon (934, 935, 935, 936, 937) without explanation, and match neither 938 nor 957.
- Section 3.4 attributes the 957→938 reduction to blocks failing quality control, but Table 3.2's sample counts equal exactly 180 × block count for every campaign, so no block was dropped. The durations (45, 24, 60, 42 min) are also exact multiples of 180 s, which is implausible for continuous field logging and suggests truncation that is not described.
- The signalling block is characterised three incompatible ways: "legitimate" and merely separated for handset-side realism (Section 4.6); performance-reducing (Section 5.8: 0.179 → 0.168); and a leak whose "leakage guard" removal "raises the apparent score substantially" (Section 5.11).
- Table 5.10, presented as "what the model uses," is headed by `sig_s_since_a3` — a feature the main model excludes.
- Section 1.5 calls the 57 blocks "57 drives"; Section 3.4 insists they are "a bookkeeping device and not a mobility unit."
- Section 4.10 computes the feasibility floor with "the 14 calibration blocks available in a held-out campaign," but campaigns have 15, 8, 20 and 14 blocks; n = 14 is one campaign, not "a held-out campaign."
- Equation numbering skips (4.10). Figure 4.1's caption line is duplicated. Section 5.4 says "Figure 5.8 places the arms on the same axes" while Figure 5.8 is the burst/quiet figure and Figure 5.7 is the one described. Figure 5.2 is placed in Section 5.1 but belongs to the event-level results of Appendix A.
- Table 3.1 lists 51 handovers "triggered by A1 instead of A3." Event A1 is a serving-becomes-better-than-threshold condition used to deactivate measurements; attributing handovers to it without comment invites the reader to doubt the attribution chain.
- References [6] and [15] carry unresolved author lists, [38] carries "confirm the version DOI before submission," and [39] carries "Venue to be confirmed." These should not reach a referee.
- Chapter 6 (outcome-based education, course outcomes, budget in BDT) is a departmental thesis requirement and has no place in a journal submission; it should be removed for Q1 submission.
- The Raca et al. dataset is described as "59 driving traces"; the MMSys 2020 release should be cited with its exact composition, since the paper's control argument depends on which subset was used.
- Section 5.10 claims the time-to-trigger is "the lever governing ping-pong, having ruled out speed as the driver," but the highway campaign — the fastest — has the *highest* ping-pong rate (32.2% vs 25.0%), which rules out neither speed nor a speed–corridor confound with a single highway session.
- No uncertainty is attached to the Event A3 baseline (0.116), so the headline "materially better than the deployed rule" is an unquantified comparison.

## Verdict

**Reject.** The alignment audit — particularly the NUWiNS end-of-second versus start-of-second manipulation on 4,712 handovers — is a genuine and transferable contribution that deserves publication, but the manuscript in its present state reports its own headline AUPRC as three different numbers, its own leaderboard two contradictory ways, its own calibration error three ways, and arithmetic in Tables 3.1 and 5.12 that cannot be correct; and its novelty framing rests on a literature screen that misses the cell-sojourn-time literature and carves out the closest prior system, so the gap the paper claims to occupy has not been established.
