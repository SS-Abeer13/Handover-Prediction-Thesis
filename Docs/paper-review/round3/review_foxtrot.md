# Review — "Leakage-Audited Multi-Horizon Handover Forecasting from LTE Drive-Test Signalling"

## Summary

The paper asks whether an LTE handover command can be forecast at 0.5–5 s lead time from handset-observable measurements, using ground truth decoded from RRC signalling rather than vendor counters. Four continuous drive-test sessions on one commercial operator in Dhaka/Gazipur (10,260 one-hertz samples, 8,586 usable rows, 957 handover commands, 2.9 h) are used. The task is posed as discrete-time survival — a single model estimates per-bin hazards and horizon probabilities follow from the product-limit identity, so horizon ordering holds by construction — and evaluated under leave-one-campaign-out (LOCO) against a ladder of three weaker splitting protocols, with expected calibration error, event-level cost, and a conformal-risk-control threshold whose exchangeable unit is a driving unit. The headline empirical result is negative-and-methodological: an alignment audit shows that in 75.9% of handovers the one-hertz export row stamped *before* the command already carries the *post*-handover serving cell, because the exporter writes each row at the end of the second it summarises; lagging every export feature one row moves one-second AUPRC from 0.600 to 0.179 (2.6× a 6.8% prevalence floor, AUROC 0.777) against 0.116 for the deployed Event A3 rule scored as a predictor. The audit's mechanism is falsified against intra-second timing and replicated by re-downsampling an independent 10 Hz XCAL corpus (93.2% contamination under an end-of-second rule, 4.1% under start-of-second on the same 4,712 events), and is absent from a sampled (non-aggregating) public log.

Let me say at the outset what is genuinely strong here, because the rest of this review presses hard. The alignment audit is the best thing in the paper and, on its own terms, is close to exemplary: it states a mechanism (Eq. 5.1), derives two falsifiable predictions from it, tests both, and finds a third dataset where the rule can be *set* rather than inferred — the last/first-sample-of-second flip on NUWiNS is a genuine experimental manipulation and is more convincing than anything else in Chapter 5. The decision to report that this finding destroyed the project's own best number (§5.11, §7.4) is creditable and rare. The splitting ladder with the parameterless A3 rule as a zero-inflation control (§5.3) is a clean design. The refusal to make a causal claim on deterministic-propensity grounds (§1.5, §5.13) is correct and well argued. The paper also pre-empts several obvious objections honestly — the cumulative-maximum control that neutralises most of the hazard-formulation's advantage (Table 5.6), the four-unit sign-test resolution floor (§4.9), the same-department provenance of the "external" dataset (§5.7, §5.13).

The problem is that almost none of the paper's *stated* claims survive contact with its own tables. Below, the concerns are ordered by how much of the paper they move.

## Major concerns

### 1. The operating points the paper measures do not support the purpose the paper gives for the system

**Issue.** §1.1 defines the system's purpose as "an indication, one or two seconds before the fact, that a handover is imminent, so that the target cell can be prepared, the transmission schedule adjusted, or an unnecessary transfer suppressed," and §1.2 asserts that "a warning one second ahead of a handover command is sufficient time for the network to complete target-cell preparation." §6.6 repeats this as the societal justification. Table A.2 is the only place in the paper where the system is scored as a warning system, and it says the opposite. At the one-second horizon: detection rate **34.0%** of commands, **179.0 false alarms per hour**, and **median lead time 0.47 s**. At 0.5 s: 21.7% detection, 277.6 false alarms/h, median lead 0.25 s. At 5 s, the most permissive row: 42.5% detection, 69.6 false alarms/h, median lead 3.31 s.

Three things follow, and the paper draws none of them. (a) 179 false alarms/h is one false alarm every 20 seconds on a route where a real handover occurs every 11 seconds — the warning is asserted for a large fraction of wall-clock time, and §5.5's *certified* operating points are far worse still (alarm on 47% of rows at α=0.20, 86% at α=0.05). (b) A median lead time of 0.47 s at the 1 s horizon is directly contradicted by the paper's own cited authority: §2.8 invokes Deb et al. to argue that "a model that identifies the correct target cell two hundred milliseconds before the event buys nothing, because the preparation message must be sent, acknowledged and stored." By that standard the 0.5 s and 1 s operating points are below the usefulness threshold the paper itself imports, and only the 5 s row clears it — at 42.5% detection. (c) §1.2's claim that one second suffices for target-cell preparation carries no citation and is not measured anywhere; the conditional-handover literature treats preparation as a multi-message, multi-hundred-millisecond budget (see e.g. [Conditional Handover in 5G – Principles, Future Use Cases and FR2 Performance](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.01283) and [On the Modeling and Analysis of Fast Conditional Handover for 5G-Advanced](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.06909)), which is precisely why fast-CHO variants exist.

There is a further internal contradiction about *which* operating point is being reported. Appendix A's prose says "Table A.2 gives the event-level operating characteristics of the primary model **at the certified operating point of Section 5.5**"; the table's own caption says "**at the 5% false-positive operating point**"; the List of Tables (p. 71 entry) says "at the certified operating point of Section 5.5"; Figure 5.2's caption says "at the 5% false-positive operating point." These are not the same point and cannot both produce Table A.2's numbers — §5.5's certified thresholds alarm on 36–86% of rows, which is nowhere near a 5% false-positive rate.

**Where.** §1.1, §1.2, §2.8, §6.6; Table A.2 and its caption vs. the Appendix A prose; Figure 5.2 caption; Table 5.7; §5.12 (O1 declared "met in a qualified form").

**Why it matters.** This is the paper's motivating claim, its societal-impact argument (§6.6), and the basis on which O1 and O3 are declared met. If the system's usable lead time at the horizons it names is shorter than the preparation budget, and its false-alarm rate at any certified threshold is of the same order as the event rate itself, then the paper has measured a ranking signal, not a warning system — which is a defensible and publishable finding, but it is not the finding the introduction and conclusion advertise. A Q1 reader in this area will go straight to Table A.2 and stop there.

**What would address it.** (i) Resolve which operating point Table A.2 reports and label it unambiguously; report *both* — the 5% FPR point and each certified α — in one table with detection rate, false alarms/h, alarm rate, and the full lead-time distribution (not only the median: the 10th/25th percentiles decide usability). (ii) State explicitly, in §1.2 and again in §5.12, the lead-time and false-alarm budget a network action actually requires, with a citation, and evaluate against it. (iii) Rewrite the O1/O3 verdicts and §6.6 to match: at present the honest statement is that no measured operating point in this paper is deployable for target-cell preparation, and that the 5 s horizon is the only one whose lead time clears the paper's own cited threshold. (iv) Table A.2's detection rate is also non-monotone in horizon (318 detected at 1 s, 314 at 2 s) despite a coherent hazard model and a rising resolvability ceiling — explain this, because as printed it suggests the operating point is being re-chosen per horizon in a way that is not described.

### 2. The conformal guarantee is not evaluated against the estimand the paper says it certifies, and one reported row is infeasible under the paper's own bound

**Issue.** §1.3 requirement (iv) states the target as "a stated bound on **the fraction of handovers it will miss** on future units," and §1.6 contribution 5 and §7.1 repeat it. What §4.10 actually certifies is
`R_d(λ) = { t ∈ d : y_t = 1 ∧ p̂_t < λ }`
— a set of **rows**, per unit. Three problems compound.

(a) *Wrong unit.* A row-level miss rate is not a handover miss rate. §3.6 reports that only **64.6%** of handover commands have any usable row inside the one-second window before them; 35.4% of events are therefore unmissable-by-construction — they cannot enter R_d at all, because they contribute no positive row. A certified row-miss rate of 0.168 at α=0.20 (Table 5.7) is therefore consistent with an event-level miss rate above 35%, and indeed Table A.2 shows event-level misses of 66% at 1 s. The paper never reconciles Table 5.7 with Table A.2, and the two are the same system at different thresholds measured in different units. The estimand the operator cares about (and which §1.3 promises) is not the estimand the bound covers.

(b) *The loss as printed is not a bounded risk.* Eq. (4.9) requires a bounded, non-increasing loss with `B = 1`, and the guarantee is `E[R_{n+1}(λ̂)] ≤ α`. The displayed `R_d` is an unnormalised cardinality with an unclosed brace — it is neither bounded by 1 nor a rate. The text calls it a "mean per-unit miss rate," so presumably the implementation divides by the unit's positive count, but that is not what is written, and with `B = 1` the distinction is the whole guarantee. Relatedly, a unit with zero positive rows makes the per-unit rate undefined; nothing says how such units are handled in `R̂`. Equation (4.10) is missing entirely from the numbering, which suggests a deleted step.

(c) *An infeasible row is reported as though it were satisfied.* §4.10 derives the feasibility floor `α ≥ 1/(n+1)`, and Table 5.7 states it as **α ≥ 0.067** for n = 14. Table 5.7's first row is **α = 0.05**, below that floor, and its "Across campaigns: rotations respecting the bound" cell reads **100%**. §5.5 then concedes that "only 25% of the rotations can express α = 0.05 at all." A 100%-compliance figure computed over rotations that cannot express the target is not evidence about the procedure; it is an artefact of a vacuous threshold (alarm on 86% of rows). This row should not be in the table in its current form.

**Where.** §1.3(iv), §1.6 item 5, §4.10 (Eq. 4.9, the `R_d` definition, Eq. 4.11, missing 4.10), Table 5.7 and §5.5 paragraphs 1 and 3, §7.1, Table A.2.

**Why it matters.** The distribution-free guarantee is one of six stated contributions and the basis for declaring O3 met. As reported, it certifies a quantity (per-drive row-miss rate at an alarm rate of 47–86%) that no operator would recognise as the promised "fraction of handovers missed," and one of its five rows is below its own feasibility floor. This is the single most technically consequential claim-vs-evidence gap after concern 1.

**What would address it.** Define the loss explicitly as a normalised, bounded, non-increasing quantity and print it correctly; state the convention for units with no positive rows. Then either (i) re-run CRC with an **event-level** miss loss — a handover counts as caught if any row in its lead window alarms — which is the estimand §1.3 promises and is still a bounded monotone loss, or (ii) restate §1.3(iv), §1.6 and §7.1 in row-level language and add an explicit paragraph reconciling Table 5.7 with Table A.2. Drop or asterisk the α = 0.05 row. Also run CRC at more than the one-second horizon, since O3 claims calibrated, certified outputs across horizons.

### 3. The headline LOCO numbers in Table 5.4 and Table 5.5 contradict each other, and the abstract's 48% figure is computed against a number that does not appear in the ladder

**Issue.** Tables 5.4 and 5.5 both report one-second AUPRC under leave-one-campaign-out, and §5.3 states the ladder experiment holds "the data, the lagged features, the formulation, the hyper-parameters, the seeds" fixed. The LOCO columns disagree for every learner:

| Learner | Table 5.4 (LOCO) | Table 5.5 (LOCO) |
|---|---|---|
| LightGBM | **0.179** | **0.160** |
| Logistic regression | **0.130** | **0.167** |
| MLP | 0.173 | 0.152 |
| Transformer | 0.152 | 0.126 |
| GRU | 0.130 | 0.125 |
| TCN | 0.167 | 0.165 |

Logistic regression moves by +28% and LightGBM by −11% between two tables of the same quantity. This is not a rounding artefact and it reverses the paper's conclusions. §5.3 asserts "Under leave-one-campaign-out the leading learner at one second is logistic regression; under random-row splitting it is LightGBM" — which is true in Table 5.5 and flatly false in Table 5.4, where LightGBM (0.179) beats logistic regression (0.130) under LOCO by the largest margin in the table. The claim that the protocol "reorders the leaderboard" — contribution 3, objective O2, and one of the two findings in §7.1 — rests entirely on which of these two tables is correct.

The abstract compounds it: "gradient-boosted trees reach AUPRC 0.179 at one second… Splitting rows at random instead inflates **the same number** by 48%." 48% is 0.236/0.160 from Table 5.5. It is not 0.236/0.179 (32%), and 0.179 × 1.48 = 0.265, which appears nowhere. The abstract's two numbers come from different tables.

**Where.** Table 5.4 vs Table 5.5; §5.2 paragraph 3; §5.3 paragraphs 3–4; abstract; §1.6 contribution 3; §5.12 (O2); §7.1.

**Why it matters.** The primary reported performance figure (0.179) and the primary protocol finding (48% inflation, architecture-dependent reordering) are mutually inconsistent as printed. If the discrepancy is a transcription error, it is a serious one in the two most-cited tables. If it reflects two different pipeline configurations — e.g. Table 5.4 pooling out-of-fold predictions across campaigns and Table 5.5 averaging per-fold AUPRCs, which would explain a discrepancy of roughly this size — then §5.3's "everything held fixed" is untrue and the inflation percentages are not controlled comparisons.

**What would address it.** Reconcile the two tables, state exactly how out-of-fold AUPRC is aggregated (pooled scores vs. mean of per-fold values) in §4.9, and recompute every inflation percentage and every ranking claim from a single source. If the aggregation differs by design, report both conventions in one table. Until this is resolved, no ranking claim in §5.2, §5.3, §5.12 or §7.1 can be assessed.

### 4. The calibration claims are over-stated, internally inconsistent, and the one operational interpretation offered is not supported by the data

**Issue.** Three different values are reported for the same quantity — the primary model's one-second ECE: **0.012** (Table 5.3), **0.036** (Table 5.6, "hazard" arm), and per-campaign 0.012–0.033 (Table 5.8). §5.4 then writes: "The operational meaning of an expected calibration error of **0.024** at the one-second horizon…" — a fourth value that appears in no table.

Worse is the interpretation attached to it: "it means that among the samples on which the system asserts a probability near 0.8, a handover follows within one second on approximately 80% of occasions." Nothing in the paper supports this. At 6.8% prevalence, AUPRC 0.179 and AUROC 0.777, the model's predicted-probability distribution is concentrated near the base rate; it is very unlikely ever to assert 0.8, and the paper never shows a reliability diagram with bin occupancy (Figure 5.6 is described only as "calibration error at one second, one line per held-out campaign"). ECE over 15 equal-mass bins on a 6.8%-prevalence task is dominated by the low-probability bins, where a constant base-rate predictor scores near zero. A low ECE here is close to uninformative, and inferring behaviour in a high-confidence bin from a mass-weighted average is exactly the inference ECE does not license. This sentence is then used to declare O3 met and to claim superiority over all 22 audited models.

Separately, §5.4 reports honestly that "the hazard arm has the lower calibration error on **0 of the four** held-out campaigns at one second," yet §7.1 states without that caveat: "The hazard model calibrates better than the projections that cost nothing (0.036 against 0.047 at one second)." A pooled statistic that reverses on every individual unit is a Simpson-type artefact, and the conclusion should not assert the pooled direction unqualified.

**Where.** Table 5.3 (ECE column), Table 5.6, Table 5.8, §5.4 paragraphs 2–5 and the paragraph beginning "The operational meaning…", §5.12 (O3), §7.1.

**Why it matters.** Calibration is the property the paper claims none of the 22 comparator models reports (Table 2.1), and it is half of O3. A claim that distinguishes this work from the entire comparator literature must be stated with a single, sourced number and an interpretation the data supports.

**What would address it.** Fix the ECE to one value per (arm, horizon) and explain the Table 5.3 / Table 5.6 discrepancy. Replace the ECE-only reporting with a reliability diagram showing **bin occupancy** and the maximum predicted probability actually emitted, at 1 s and 5 s. Delete or evidence the "near 0.8" sentence. Add a calibration reference line — ECE of a constant base-rate predictor and of the A3 rule — so the reader can judge whether 0.012–0.036 is good. Carry the 0/4 per-campaign result into §7.1.

### 5. The 0.600 → 0.179 drop measures the repair, not the leak, and is reported as if it measured the leak

**Issue.** The audit establishes convincingly that the row stamped *t* is contaminated. The repair chosen is to lag **every export-derived feature** by one full row. §5.13 concedes this is "a conservative repair chosen without vendor confirmation of the write rule," and §7.2 that "every figure here is conservative by that margin." But the abstract, §1.6 contribution 1, §5.1, §5.11 and §7.1 all present the 0.600 → 0.179 delta as the cost of *the alignment defect* ("moves the one-second AUPRC of the same model from 0.600 to 0.179"; "the strongest configuration this project measured… was an artefact of a one-second misalignment").

These are different quantities. A one-row lag removes the contaminated serving-cell register *and* one full second of legitimately-available RSRP/RSRQ/SINR/speed history that is not contaminated by the aggregation rule. The contamination mechanism (Eq. 5.1) is specific to the serving-cell identity register; nothing in §5.1 shows that the radio scalars in row *t* are similarly post-hoc. The paper never runs the intermediate control: keep row *t* but drop or rebuild the serving-cell-derived fields (dwell time, PCI-change indicators, serving/neighbour assignment), which is the targeted repair the mechanism implies. Without it, 0.179 is a lower bound on achievable performance and 0.421 is an upper bound on the leak, and the true leakage cost is somewhere between.

Two smaller evidential wobbles in the same section. (i) The "chance" baseline is used inconsistently: for the authors' own data, 11.7–12.1% (isolated handovers, rows t−1/t−3) is treated as background, but the Raca control at **29.4%** is described as "there is none" — 29.4% is 2.5× the paper's own stated background and is not interrogated. (ii) The mechanism predicts that for commands in the first quarter of the second the margin `m = 1 − δ − x` is comfortably positive (δ ≤ 0.25, x ≈ 0.06), so contamination should be near-total; the measured value is **86.1%**. The paper explains the ~22.5% residual as a one-row KPI refresh lag, but then writes "the uncontaminated figure for it is not zero but about 24%," which is numerically indistinguishable from the all-handover background rate at t−3 (23.7%) and is left ambiguous as to what it refers to.

**Where.** Abstract; §1.6 contribution 1; §5.1 (Table 5.2, the residual paragraph, the Raca paragraph); §5.11; §5.13; §7.1.

**Why it matters.** Contribution 1 is the paper's strongest and most transferable result, and the number attached to it (a 70% AUPRC collapse) is the number every downstream reader will quote. Conflating "cost of the leak" with "cost of a conservative repair" overstates the field-wide implication and simultaneously understates what this system could achieve with a targeted fix.

**What would address it.** Add a third row to Table 5.2: row *t* retained, serving-cell-derived features removed or reconstructed from the signalling clock. Report the three-way comparison and restate the abstract/§1.6/§7.1 claims in terms of "what a one-row lag costs" (which Table 5.2's own caption already says correctly) versus "what the contamination is worth." Interrogate the 29.4% Raca figure against a stated null. Clarify what "about 24%" refers to.

### 6. The censoring machinery is inert on this data, yet is claimed as an advantage in three places

**Issue.** §4.1 argues at length that right censoring must be handled: "Right censoring is not a nuisance to be discarded here; the last samples of every drive are censored, and discarding them would both waste data and bias the observed event-time distribution towards short intervals." §4.4 then develops the censoring-aware likelihood (Eq. 4.6) — and immediately states: "Because every retained row carries a full five-second follow-up window inside its own campaign, **no row is censored inside the horizon grid**." Table 4.2's at-risk column confirms it: 8,586 → 8,267 → 8,003 → 7,506 → 7,073, decrementing by exactly the event counts with no censoring attrition.

So the rows §4.1 says must not be discarded *were* discarded (the row set is restricted to those with a full five-second follow-up), and the likelihood machinery that would have handled them does nothing. Yet the censoring treatment is claimed as a live advantage of the formulation in §5.4 ("It also inherits the censoring treatment of Section 4.4"), as a knowledge-profile justification in Table 6.3 (K3), and implicitly in §7.4.

**Where.** §4.1 final paragraph; §4.4 paragraph 3 and Table 4.2; §5.4 paragraph beginning "What the hazard formulation retains"; Table 6.3 (K3).

**Why it matters.** After the cumulative-maximum control removes the coherence advantage (Table 5.6) and the isotonic+PAV composite beats it on calibration, the hazard formulation's remaining case rests on exactly three things: one fit instead of five, no held-out calibration split, and censoring. One of the three is inoperative here. The reader is entitled to know that the argument is two-legged, not three.

**What would address it.** State plainly in §4.4 and §5.4 that censoring is inert on this row set and that the likelihood is retained for generality. Alternatively — and better — actually use it: retain the end-of-session rows as censored observations, which would both honour §4.1's argument and add data to a dataset the paper repeatedly calls too small. Report what that changes.

### 7. The re-establishment rate threatens the ground truth and offers an unexamined alternative explanation for the cross-campaign spread

**Issue.** §1.2 and §3.6 report **341 RRC connection re-establishment procedures** in 2.9 hours alongside 957 handover commands — one re-establishment per 30 seconds of driving, or roughly one for every 2.8 handover commands — with **321 carrying `reconfigurationFailure`** and a median occurrence **8 seconds after the previous handover command**. The paper's treatment is a single deflecting sentence: "These are predominantly failures to apply a reconfiguration rather than radio link failures during mobility, and they are treated as a competing event in Section 5.8 rather than as a motivating statistic."

This deserves far more. (a) A `reconfigurationFailure` rate of this magnitude on a live commercial network is not normal; it suggests either a device/firmware problem with the instrumented handset, a decoding artefact in the XCAL parse, or a genuinely pathological network state — and each of those has different consequences for the validity of the 957 labels. (b) The paper's label is the handover **command** (`RRCConnectionReconfiguration` with `mobilityControlInfo`), not the completed handover. If a substantial fraction of commands are followed within seconds by a failed reconfiguration and re-establishment, then a meaningful share of "handover events" in the ground truth are commands that did not result in a successful handover — which changes what the model is predicting and undermines the paper's central methodological argument that a decoded command is superior ground truth to a vendor counter (§3.1). (c) The distribution across campaigns is extreme and unexplained: Table 3.2 gives 113 / 64 / 159 / **5** re-establishments for the four campaigns. The highway campaign has essentially none while dense urban has 159. The highway campaign is also the weakest predictively (AUPRC 0.132) and the one held out as the design's generalisation test. Radio/device state differing this drastically is a live alternative explanation for the cross-campaign spread that the paper attributes to corridor and speed regime (§5.6, §3.5). (d) §5.8 reports censoring only "**93 rows** whose next event is a re-establishment" for 341 events — the relationship between 341 events and 93 rows is not explained.

**Where.** §1.2; §3.6; Table 3.2 (Re-establishments column); §5.8 final sensitivity paragraph; §5.6; §5.13.

**Why it matters.** Ground-truth quality is the paper's foundational claim ("Every claim made in this thesis rests on the quality of the measurement underneath it," §3 opening). A 36%-of-handovers re-establishment rate with a near-zero count on one of four campaigns is either a measurement pathology or a substantive network finding, and in both cases it bears directly on label validity and on the generalisation story.

**What would address it.** Report the joint distribution of commands and subsequent re-establishments: how many commands are followed by a re-establishment within 1/2/5 s; what fraction of the 957 are plausibly failed handovers; whether `RRCConnectionReconfigurationComplete` was observed for each (the paper already uses these messages in §5.1, so the data exists — it reports that only 65% of commands record a non-zero interruption, which is itself unexplained). Re-run the primary result on the subset of *confirmed-complete* handovers. Explain the highway campaign's count of 5 and test whether device/network state, rather than corridor and speed, explains the Table 5.8 spread. Reconcile 341 events with 93 rows.

### 8. The A3 conversion-rate contribution contains arithmetic that cannot be right

**Issue.** This is one of six headline contributions (§1.6 item 6) and it is reported with impossible numbers.

- §1.6: "the loose episode rule lets 2,361 episodes claim **1,647 of 957** commands." One cannot claim 1,647 of 957 objects. §5.9 repeats it: "the 2,361 episodes claim 1,647 commands between them out of the 957 that exist." 1,647 is the *episode* count in Table 5.12's "Followed by a command within 2 s" column — i.e. the number of episodes with a command nearby, not a number of commands claimed. The sentence conflates the two, and the derived "the same command is counted by 2.6 episodes on average" is 1647/635, which is episodes-with-a-command per *owned* command — not the stated quantity.
- Table 5.12, "command multiplicity" rows: "2,361 | 1647 claimed, 635 owned | **×650.00 inflation**" and "9,819 | 4391 claimed, 908 owned | **×927.00 inflation**." Neither inflation figure is a ratio of anything in its own row; 650 and 927 look like stray counts that have been formatted as multipliers.
- Table 3.1: the eleven rows sum to **907** handovers and **94.7%**, not 957 and 100%. Fifty handovers (5.3%) are unaccounted for with no row. The A3 sub-rows sum to **648**, while the summary line asserts "**651** A3 total."
- §5.9 states "651 of 957 handover commands are attributed to an A3 report," while the one-to-one rule yields **635** episodes owning a command. If each command has exactly one attributed report and each episode owns at most one command, these should agree. They differ by 16 and the discrepancy is not addressed.
- §2.4 previews the ping-pong ladder as "a single fixed set of **938** handovers, where the rate moves from **24.5% to 41.3%**"; §5.10 and Table 5.13 report **957** handovers and **26.3% to 43.8%**, and the grid as "between **21.0% and 43.8%**." Three different ranges for the same experiment.
- §3.4 states that "the 957 figure is used only where the raw event stream is the appropriate object, which in this thesis occurs in **exactly one place**: the ungrouped variant of the ping-pong definition ladder in Section **5.9**." Ping-pong is §5.10; Table 5.13 uses 957 in three of its four rows; and the same paragraph two sentences earlier says "the event-level analyses of Sections 5.9 and 5.10 use all 957 commands."

**Where.** §1.6 item 6; Table 3.1; Table 5.12 and §5.9 paragraphs 3–5; §2.4 vs §5.10 and Table 5.13; §3.4.

**Why it matters.** The paper's own thesis in this section is that the field is careless with units and denominators ("a rate quoted without its unit is a statement about bookkeeping and not about the network"). A section making that argument cannot itself print "1,647 of 957," a ×650 inflation factor, a configuration table that sums to 94.7%, and three different ranges for one ladder. Reviewers will read this as evidence that the numbers were assembled by hand rather than regenerated, which directly contradicts Appendix C's claim that "every table reported in Chapters 4 and 5 is regenerated from the stage outputs rather than transcribed."

**What would address it.** Regenerate Table 3.1 and Table 5.12 from the pipeline; make Table 3.1 sum to 957 (add the missing category or a residual row); define "claimed" and "owned" precisely in the caption and print a ratio that is a ratio; rewrite the §1.6 and §5.9 sentences in terms of episodes and commands consistently; reconcile 635 vs 651 and 648 vs 651; align §2.4 with §5.10; fix the §3.4 self-contradiction.

### 9. Chapter 5's two "network characterisation" conclusions are drawn from variation the study does not have

**Issue.** §5.10 concludes: "The lever available to an operator is therefore the **time-to-trigger and offset** of the dominant configuration profile rather than any speed-dependent adaptation." But §3.5 establishes that "the configuration set is **stable across all four campaigns**" — there is no variation in TTT or offset anywhere in this dataset. The conclusion is a causal-flavoured claim about a parameter the study never varied, inferred purely by elimination from two cross-sectional stratifications. And the elimination does not work: Table 5.14 shows the highway campaign (≈2× mean speed) at **32.2%** ping-pong against 25.0% urban, which is a *positive* association with speed, yet §5.10 reads this as "It is not driven by speed." Higher-with-speed is not evidence of no speed effect; it is weak evidence of one, confounded with corridor, cell density and the re-establishment anomaly of concern 7. §7.3 then escalates the inference into a recommended experiment ("Section 5.10 identifies the time-to-trigger… as the lever, **having ruled out speed as the driver**").

The Hawkes analysis is offered as corroboration — "Modelling the arrival stream as a self-exciting process supports the same conclusion from a different direction" — but self-excitation of the arrival stream says nothing about TTT or offset; it is a non sequitur as a support for a configuration-lever claim. And the Hawkes fit is itself reported as rejected: "the Ogata residual test rejects the exponential kernel." The paper is to be credited for reporting the rejection, but it then interprets the branching ratio from the rejected model as a physical quantity ("each handover command is followed on average by roughly 0.7 further commands attributable to it") and builds a learner on that kernel (Table 5.4, "Hawkes-kernel intensity (LR)", AUPRC 0.120). α/β from a misspecified kernel is not an interpretable branching ratio.

**Where.** §5.10 paragraphs 4–6; Table 5.14; §3.5; §7.3 item 5; §4.11; Figures 5.17–5.18.

**Why it matters.** This is half of contribution 6 and the substantive (non-methodological) part of O5. It also feeds §6.6's environmental-impact argument. A conclusion about a parameter that was never varied, supported by a stratification that points the other way and a point-process fit the paper reports as rejected, is not a finding.

**What would address it.** Restate the ping-pong conclusion as descriptive: the phenomenon concentrates in intra-frequency transfers (27.1% vs 7.2%) and is not lower at highway speed. Remove the "lever" and "ruled out speed" language, or support it with actual parameter variation (which §7.3 correctly identifies as future work — so it cannot also be a present finding). Either fit a kernel the residual test does not reject, or report the branching ratio explicitly as a summary of a rejected fit and withdraw the "0.7 offspring per event" interpretation and the Hawkes learner's standing as a baseline.

### 10. Statistical treatment: n = 4 units, no uncertainty on any comparison that a conclusion depends on

**Issue.** The paper is admirably explicit that four sessions bound what can be said (§3.6, §4.9, §5.6, §7.2), and that the block-bootstrap intervals are "conditional on these four sessions." It then proceeds to draw conclusions that those intervals cannot support. Table 5.3's headline interval for one-second AUPRC is [0.159, 0.204] — a width of 0.045 — while Table 5.8's per-campaign values run 0.132 to 0.233, a spread of 0.101, more than twice as wide. The narrow interval is the one quoted in the abstract, §5.2 and §7.1. Tables 5.4, 5.5, 5.10, 5.11 and A.1 carry **no** uncertainty at all, yet conclusions are drawn from differences of 0.005–0.012 in them (LightGBM 0.179 vs TCN 0.167 vs MLP 0.173; "the leading learner is logistic regression"; "adding the signalling block moves AUPRC from 0.179 to 0.168"). The tuning protocol compounds this: 20 random-search trials × 7 learners × 4 protocols × 4 outer folds × 3 seeds, with no accounting for selection over that search space, on 8,586 rows containing ~584 one-second positives drawn from 938 events.

**Where.** Table 5.3 vs Table 5.8; Tables 5.4, 5.5, 5.10, 5.11, 5.15, A.1; §4.9 "Repetition and uncertainty"; §5.2 final paragraph; §5.8 final paragraph.

**Why it matters.** Two of the paper's conclusions are comparative rankings — that protocol reorders learners (O2, contribution 3), and that the signalling block hurts. Neither is given an interval. With four units and a per-campaign spread of 0.1 AUPRC, a 0.011 difference is not interpretable, and the paper elsewhere says so itself.

**What would address it.** Put per-campaign values and a paired spread beside every number in Tables 5.4, 5.5, 5.10, 5.11 and A.1 (Figure 5.4 apparently already shows this for one row — promote it into the tables). Quote the wider, per-campaign spread in the abstract and §7.1 rather than the conditional bootstrap interval, or quote both. State whether the random search was re-run inside every fold of every protocol; if not, the ladder comparison inherits a selection made under one protocol.

### 11. The novelty claims rest on an audit whose protocol is unstated and whose own table records unverified rows — and the nearest prior system is excluded from it and never benchmarked

**Issue.** Several of the paper's strongest claims are "no one has done X" claims: §1.3 ("None of these five requirements is satisfied jointly anywhere in the audited literature, and the fourth and fifth are not satisfied anywhere at all in the handover-prediction setting"); Table 2.1's 0/22 rows; §2.9; Table 6.5 attribute A3 ("neither of which appears in the audited literature"). All depend on the audit of 22 models drawn from "108 publications screened." The screening protocol is not reported anywhere: no databases, no search strings, no date range, no inclusion/exclusion criteria, no PRISMA-style flow — this in a paper that explicitly praises Ankome and Hanada for screening under a PRISMA protocol (§2.3).

Appendix D then undercuts the audit from inside. Row 11 records split protocol "NOT VERIFIED", prevalence-aware metric "not verified", calibration "not verified". Row 7 records data as "UNVERIFIED 'real-world'". Rows 15, 20, 21 and 22 record "n/a – no models trained", "n/a (RL)", "n/a (no learning)" and "n/a (regression)" — so the denominator of 22 includes at least one dataset paper that trains no model and one purely analytical study. Table 2.1's "0/22" and "2/22" counts are therefore computed over a denominator containing non-models and rows the authors state they could not verify, and are used to support universal negative claims.

The most important gap is specific. §2.2 and §2.3 identify Prognos, inside Hassan et al. ([Vivisecting mobility management in 5G cellular networks](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544216.3544217), SIGCOMM 2022; [PDF](https://feng-qian.github.io/paper/5g_mobility_sigcomm22.pdf)), as "the closest prior system to the one built here: it predicts handovers from XCAL-decoded RRC state on a live network and reports a lead-time gain of roughly 900 ms." It is then **excluded** from the audit on the grounds that it is "a system component rather than a published prediction model with its own protocol," and it is never reimplemented, never scored, and never appears in any results table. That is the wrong way round: the closest prior system is precisely the one a Q1 reviewer expects as a baseline, and its released [artifact](https://github.com/SIGCOMM22-5GMobility/artifact) makes at least a partial comparison feasible. As it stands, the paper's system achieves a median lead time of 0.47 s at 1 s (Table A.2) and never places that beside the ~900 ms it attributes to Prognos.

Similarly, the claim that discrete-time survival "has not been applied to mobility events" (§2.5, §2.9) is stated against an audit of *prediction* papers, without engaging the substantial analytical literature that models cell residence and handover as a time-to-event/sojourn-time problem (e.g. [Handover Rate and Sojourn Time Analysis in Mobile Networks](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.05019)) or the survey literature on discrete-time deep survival models the paper's own framing sits inside ([An Introduction to Deep Survival Analysis Models for Predicting Time-to-Event Outcomes](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01086v1)). The paper is careful to disclaim novelty for the formulation itself (§2.5) — good — but §2.9 and Table 6.5 A3 then reclaim it as application novelty without surveying the analytical handover-sojourn literature at all.

**Where.** §1.3 final sentence; §2.3 (including the Prognos exclusion paragraph); Table 2.1; §2.5; §2.9; Appendix D rows 7, 11, 15, 20, 21, 22; Table 6.5 A3.

**Why it matters.** Universal negatives ("none of 22," "not satisfied anywhere at all") are the strongest form of claim a paper can make, and they need the strongest evidence. An unreported screening protocol plus self-declared unverified rows cannot carry them, and excluding the acknowledged nearest system from the comparison set is the kind of scoping decision that reads as convenient.

**What would address it.** Report the screening protocol in full (databases, strings, dates, criteria, counts at each stage) as supplementary material. Remove non-model entries from the denominator or report two denominators. Either verify rows 7 and 11 or exclude them and say so. Most importantly: benchmark against Prognos, or state concretely what prevents it (data access, task mismatch) and what partial comparison is possible — the lead-time comparison in particular. Add a short survey of analytical handover sojourn-time/residence-time models in §2.5.

### 12. Reproducibility and manuscript integrity fall short of what the paper demands of others

**Issue.** Table 2.1 scores the comparator literature on "Releases code: 2/22" and "Releases data: 1/22" and enters "**Planned**" for this work in both cells. Scoring one's own intentions in a column that records others' actions is not a like-for-like comparison, and §7.3 concedes that "such claims are only worth making if others can reproduce and contest them." Appendix C describes a stage structure but gives no repository, environment, seeds, hyperparameter search ranges, or dataset identifier for the authors' own captures; the pre-registration manifest (§3.2, Appendix C) is "auditable but not independently timestamped."

The manuscript also does not appear to have been through a final consistency pass, which matters here because the paper's argument is that careless bookkeeping invalidates results:

- **Feature counts.** §2.10 Stage 3: "One hundred and fifty-two candidate features." §4.6: "three blocks and **112 columns** in total"; Table 4.3 lists 83 + 17 + 6 = **106** but labels the main set **112**. §4.7: "a regularised linear model over the **107** features." Appendix B: "The **107** features retained" then enumerates 83 + 17 + 13 + 7 = **120**, with signalling at 13 (Table 4.3 says 10) and history at 7 (Table 4.3 says 6). No two agree.
- **References.** The in-text numbering drifts from the reference list starting at [21]: §2.6 cites "Cohen, Park, Simeone and Shamai [18]" but [18] is Candès et al.; "Conformal risk control… is due to Angelopoulos et al. Reference [36] (Kaufman et al. on leakage) is never cited in the text.
- **Reference list contains editorial placeholders**: [6] "[Author list to be completed from the ACM record before submission]"; [15] "[Full author list to be completed…]"; [38] "[confirm the version DOI before submission]"; [39] "[Venue to be confirmed; a ResearchGate record exists]".

## Minor concerns

- **Conflict of interest handled well but incompletely.** §5.7, §5.13 and §6.6 disclose that the "independent" public dataset [38] and the reimplemented comparator [39] were produced in the same department by authors including this thesis's supervisor. That disclosure is exactly right. But the comparator, which §5.13 says "proved close to uninformative" and was "reported in Section 5.11," does **not** appear in Table 5.15 or anywhere else in Chapter 5. A negative result about a colleague's method must be shown, not asserted, and the manuscript needs a formal COI statement in the front matter rather than a sentence in threats-to-validity.
- **§5.7 over-reads a tiny sample.** The public-domain test set is 1,455 rows at 6.3% prevalence — about 92 positives. That the transferred model (AUROC 0.760) beats the model trained on that data (0.711) is far more plausibly small-sample noise than transfer success; the paper says "the intervals overlap" but then reports the ordering as informative. Also, both domains are rebuilt from L3 signalling alone, so §5.7 validates a *different* model (AUROC 0.730 in-domain) from the one Chapter 5 reports (0.777). This should be stated where O4 is declared met (§5.12).
- **§5.6's "weakest campaign" claim is inconsistent with its own table.** Table 5.8 makes 15 Sept weakest by AUPRC (0.132) but 10 Sept weakest by lift (2.4×) and by AUROC (0.765). The text asserts 15 Sept is weakest and separately highlights its AUROC of 0.769 as reassuring — pick one metric and be consistent.
- **Pooled ECE (0.012, Table 5.8) is lower than every per-campaign ECE** (0.018, 0.021, 0.012, 0.033). Possible under pooling, but combined with Table 5.6's 0.036 it needs a sentence of explanation.
- **AUROC is identical to three decimals at 0.5 s and 1 s (0.777, Table 5.3)** — worth confirming this is not a copy error.
- **Table 4.3 vs §4.6 body:** §4.6 says the history block is computed "over the whole continuous session rather than within a 180-second block," which means history features cross block boundaries; since blocks are the bootstrap resampling unit and the CRC calibration unit, this creates dependence between "exchangeable" units that §4.10's guarantee assumes away. Worth an explicit sentence.
- **§4.10 sign convention.** With λ "ordered by decreasing strictness" and R non-increasing, `λ̂ = inf{λ : …}` as written selects the most permissive feasible threshold; confirm the direction and state it, since it determines whether the reported alarm rates are minimal.
- **Equation (4.10) is missing** from the numbering (4.9 is followed by 4.11).
- **§4.8 lists three calibration treatments** but temperature scaling appears in no results table.
- **Table 5.15 lists "Sequence models… No gain"** but Table A.1 shows TCN beating LightGBM at 5 s (0.481 vs 0.475) and 3 s (0.367 vs 0.362). "No gain at 1 s" would be accurate; "no gain" is not.
- **Acronym list** includes CHO, HOM, QoE, KPI and RLF, several of which are barely or never used; conversely CRC, PAV and CORAL usage is uneven.
- **§1.2's "8,136 A3 reports … 635 own a handover command"** is stated in the Motivation before the unit problem is introduced in §5.9; forward-reference it or it reads as a settled rate.
- **Figure 5.13 is described as showing a relation "monotone up to about twenty seconds and flat thereafter"** but no numbers accompany it; given that this dose-response is the mechanism behind the history block, it warrants a table row.
- **§5.11's final sentence** ("removing the signalling leakage guard… raises the apparent score substantially") gives no number, in a section whose purpose is to report negative results quantitatively. Quantify it.

## Verdict

**Major revision.** The alignment audit is a genuine, well-falsified, transferable contribution and the paper's methodological honesty is above the norm for this literature — but as submitted the two headline LOCO tables contradict each other on every learner, the conformal guarantee certifies a row-level quantity while the text promises an event-level one (with one reported target below its own feasibility floor), no measured operating point supports the target-cell-preparation motivation the introduction and impact chapter rest on, and a headline contribution contains arithmetic ("1,647 of 957," "×650.00 inflation," a configuration table summing to 94.7%) that cannot be right; none of this is fatal to the underlying work, but all of it must be resolved — and the code and data actually released — before the paper's claims can be assessed, let alone accepted.
