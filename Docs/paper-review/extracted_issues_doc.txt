[0] (Title) LTE handover research review
[1] (Normal) Issues questions and proposed corrections
20 September 2026
[2] (Normal) The research direction remains defensible: predict future handover commands from measurements genuinely available before the command. The present evidence does not yet validate every reported feature, physical warning time or statistical guarantee. Resolve data provenance and timing before treating the final model scores as settled.
[3] (Normal) This document consolidates the discussion of the revised thesis, both Python notebooks and eight collected data files. It records confirmed findings, unresolved questions and proposed solutions. No model was retrained and no manuscript or notebook was modified during this review.
[4] (Heading 2) Evidence and scope
[5] (Normal) Reviewed: Handover_Thesis_Manuscript_Revised.docx, with the earlier Revised-4 version used for comparison; handover_pipeline.ipynb; results_visualisation.ipynb; and the CSV and signalling TXT files for 10, 12, 13 and 15 September 2026. Notebook cell references below are zero-based.
[6] (Heading 2) What is already supported
[7] (Normal) The raw audit independently finds 957 reconfiguration messages containing mobilityControlInfo and 341 re-establishment requests. All 957 commands have a subsequent transaction-ID-matching completion within two seconds in file order. This reduces the earlier concern that completion filtering concealed extra unmatched commands in these texts. It does not prove the capture contains every physical attempt.
[8] (Heading 2) Priority order
[9] (Normal) First resolve timing and the origin of neighbor features. Next rebuild a reproducible feature table and evaluation. Then regenerate results and remove conflicting manuscript passages. New architectures and stronger claims should wait until these checks pass.
[11] (Heading 1) 1 Timing questions and prediction validity
[12] (Heading 2) One second sampling and delayed event labels
[13] (Normal) Confirmed: the CSV is approximately one hertz. A raw PCI-only check finds the target PCI already in the command-second row for 728 of 957 commands, or 76.1%. This supports a mismatch in recorded timing, but does not identify its sole cause.
[14] (Normal) Question: Does the CSV summarize later measurements, does PC signalling time lag the event, or do both happen?
[15] (Normal) Solution: Verify timestamp semantics separately for each source. Report the empirical mismatch now; treat end-of-second aggregation and delayed signalling as candidate explanations until independently checked.
[16] (Heading 2) Physical prediction versus prediction of a delayed log entry
[17] (Normal) Unresolved: actual event time and logging/availability time may differ. Hypothetically, an event at 0.2 s, a post-event feature at 0.4 s and a label at 0.7 s create an apparent 0.3 s warning even though the event already happened.
[18] (Normal) Question: At what time could the intended live predictor obtain each input, and what exact event does its label represent?
[19] (Normal) Solution: Define the target as command issuance, completion or another explicit event. Store event time and availability time separately when recoverable. Features must be available at the decision time. Evaluate physical lead time only against a validated event clock.
[20] (Heading 2) The internal counter and compressed PC timestamps
[21] (Normal) Confirmed: 742 of 957 ordered command/completion matches are only 0–1 ms apart in PC time. Multiple messages share timestamps. The internal Time counter has an observed elapsed rate near 32,768 units per second; its semantics have not been established.
[22] (Normal) Question: Is the counter a suitable event clock? Are there resets, wraparounds, drift or variable PC delay?
[23] (Normal) Solution: Compare counter increments with PC time within sessions, preserve file order for timestamp ties, and obtain counter/export documentation or native-rate evidence. Event-driven recording does not imply that the underlying clock advances only at events. Do not infer physical interruption or 50–75 ms update delays from unvalidated PC timing.
[24] (Heading 2) Whether lagging one row is sufficient
[25] (Normal) Unresolved: a one-row lag removes much suspicious information but also makes valid information older. The score drop alone cannot separate those effects.
[26] (Normal) Question: Does the lag cover the largest relevant timing mismatch for every feature family?
[27] (Normal) Solution: Run fixed-fold sensitivity analyses using several feature lags and plausible event-time offsets. Select the alignment from independent timing evidence, not the highest prediction score. Present unresolved timing as a limitation; longer horizons do not automatically eliminate leakage.
[29] (Heading 1) 2 Irregular measurements and missing fields
[30] (Heading 2) Whether machine learning needs continuous measurements
[31] (Normal) Clarification: ML does not require continuous or equally spaced measurements. It requires a causal record of what was known at each prediction time. Signalling messages naturally arrive irregularly.
[32] (Normal) Question: Can a prediction be made when no new signalling message has arrived?
[33] (Normal) Solution: Yes. Use a one-second decision grid with the latest available observations, their ages and missingness flags. Update report counts and time-since-event features causally. No new report means no newer observation; it does not prove unchanged radio conditions. Never interpolate using a future report.
[34] (Heading 2) Neighbor feature provenance
[35] (Normal) Confirmed: all Best_N1, Best_N2 and Best_N3 fields are empty in every supplied CSV. The user reports that their XCAL setup cannot record these periodic neighbor measurements. Manuscript §4.6 nevertheless assigns neighbor features to the one-hertz export.
[36] (Normal) Question: What generated the 31 neighbor/gap features, the gap AUROC, A3 baseline and neighbor curves in Figures 1.1 and 5.20?
[37] (Normal) Solution: Trace each field to its actual source. If reconstructed from measurement reports, label it report-derived, use past reports only, retain observation age and reset or expire stale values after relevant state changes. If no source exists, remove the feature and its interpretations and rerun affected results. Report-derived neighbors also change the meaning of the claimed signalling-free main model.
[38] (Heading 2) Duplicate radio field names
[39] (Normal) Confirmed: RSRP, RSRQ, RSSI and RI appear twice in the CSV header. The two RSRP columns differ on 7,124 of 10,106 jointly populated rows, about 70.5%.
[40] (Normal) Question: Which occurrence feeds training, the baseline and the plots, and what does each represent?
[41] (Normal) Solution: Assign unique names using original column position and documented measurement meaning. Keep an explicit source-to-feature dictionary. Do not let parser defaults silently select a family or average different measurement types.
[42] (Heading 2) Sentinels and mobility measurement quality
[43] (Normal) Confirmed: the Ant1 LL1 RSSI field contains 351 values of −204. Other antenna fields contain extreme values. GPS is absent for the first 170 rows on 12 September and 82 rows on 15 September. Heading is not an explicit CSV column. Some coordinate steps imply speeds above 150 km/h.
[44] (Normal) Question: Are unavailable antenna values treated as signals? Are GPS gaps filled with future values or interpreted as stops?
[45] (Normal) Solution: Verify sentinel meanings and convert unavailable values to missing. Use bounded causal filling, age indicators and quality flags. Derive heading from valid past fixes and suppress unreliable estimates near stationarity. Do not bridge large GPS gaps or smooth using future coordinates.
[47] (Heading 1) 3 Labels configuration and network interpretations
[48] (Heading 2) Configuration replay starts with incomplete knowledge
[49] (Normal) Confirmed: 2, 14, 1 and 4 reports occur before the first captured measConfig fragment on the four respective days. A first fragment may itself be incomplete.
[50] (Normal) Question: How are those reports classified, and how does replay handle removals, inter-frequency changes and connection resets?
[51] (Normal) Solution: Maintain an unresolved category. Never resolve an early report using a later binding as if it were known earlier. Replay identifiers and removals in order with explicit connection context and apply the relevant TS 36.331 rules [1]. Retain a trace showing which configuration classified each report.
[52] (Heading 2) A3 reports are not identical to new handover opportunities
[53] (Normal) Confirmed: the TXT includes repeated reporting, reportAmount infinity and reportOnLeave TRUE. The profile at 15 September 12:28:30.111 includes positive and negative A3 offsets with different reporting behavior.
[54] (Normal) Question: Does an episode represent an entry, repeated reports, a leave report or overlapping observations of several cells?
[55] (Normal) Solution: Define episodes by documented configuration and cell context, with time-gap sensitivity checks. Decode offset and hysteresis units correctly. Do not call the lack of an exported neighbor proof that A3 was impossible; periodic neighbor fields are empty throughout these files. TS 36.331 defines reporting behavior and half-decibel parameter scaling [1].
[56] (Heading 2) Report association does not establish a network decision
[57] (Normal) Unresolved: matching the most recent report within two seconds does not prove it caused the command. The reported 1,647 loose claims, 635 owning episodes and 651 associated commands use different units.
[58] (Normal) Question: Does the associated report contain the target cell and carrier? How are ties and multiple commands per episode handled?
[59] (Normal) Solution: Use target and carrier consistency where possible; retain ambiguous and unmatched cases. Publish the matching rule and reconcile reports, episodes and unique commands. Replace “declined”, “triggered by” and “monitoring purpose” with descriptive association wording unless independent evidence supports intent or causality.
[60] (Heading 2) Cell identity failures and service benefit
[61] (Normal) Confirmed: re-establishment causes are 321 reconfigurationFailure, 17 otherFailure and 3 handoverFailure. The highway TXT also contains GSM/EGPRS traffic. Global cell identity and RRC-state CSV fields are sparse.
[62] (Normal) Question: Are state histories carried across releases or RAT changes? Does a return to a PCI actually identify the same cell and an unnecessary transfer?
[63] (Normal) Solution: Reconstruct connection episodes and censor or reset history at gaps. Use PCI plus carrier at minimum and global identity where resolvable; document remaining ambiguity. Distinguish observed returns from unnecessary handovers, and command prediction from failure prevention. Empty RTT fields and one-second throughput cannot establish brief service interruptions or saved latency.
[65] (Heading 1) 4 Evaluation population and operating cost
[66] (Normal) Confirmed policy effect: every one of the 957 commands has a raw CSV timestamp in its preceding one-second window. The audit below applies only a two-second post-command blank; it does not reproduce all model feature-validity or block-boundary exclusions.
[67] (Heading 2) The eligible window ceiling
[68] (Normal) Confirmed: blanking alone removes the one-second prediction opportunity for 343/957 commands, or 35.8%. This is close to the manuscript’s missing-event share, but uses a different raw population.
[69] (Normal) Question: How much exclusion comes from blanking, missing inputs, history requirements and boundaries separately?
[70] (Normal) Solution: Publish a row and event exclusion ledger. Call this an eligible-window limit under the selected policy. Report detection over all events and eligible events, and compare blank durations. Do not call it an intrinsic one-hertz or physical predictability ceiling.
[71] (Heading 2) Campaign independence and the highway test
[72] (Normal) Confirmed: there are four collected sessions, not 57 independent drives. Carrier mix, missingness and other-RAT traffic differ between campaigns. The alignment audit used all four days.
[73] (Normal) Question: Was the highway session genuinely untouched by every modelling and analysis choice? What does the version history establish?
[74] (Normal) Solution: Use campaign-level outer evaluation and tune only inside training campaigns. Treat adjacent blocks as dependent. State exactly what was frozen and what changed later. Describe highway transfer across multiple changing conditions; the experiment does not isolate speed. Obtain an untouched session for stronger confirmation.
[75] (Heading 2) Comparisons and operational usefulness
[76] (Normal) Reported results: AUPRC 0.179 at 1 s and 0.475 at 5 s; event detection about 34% at 179 false alarms/hour and 42.5% at 69.6/hour. These were not independently retrained.
[77] (Normal) Question: Are all comparisons on identical rows, folds and tuning budgets? Are alarms actionable at these costs?
[78] (Normal) Solution: Keep experiment arms separate. Specify whether false-positive rate is per row, alarm episode or event; define cooldown and matching rules. Compare at equal alarm cost and report lead-time distributions. A one-hertz A3 margin is a proxy, not a reconstruction of a 160–640 ms timer. Sequence models using ten rows need a tabular history comparator with equivalent information.
[80] (Heading 1) 5 Calibration risk control and statistical claims
[81] (Heading 2) Calibration and baseline value
[82] (Normal) Concern: a low pooled ECE does not guarantee reliable high-probability predictions or calibration on every campaign. At the reported 1 s prevalence of 0.068, a constant predictor has Brier score 0.068 × 0.932 = 0.063376; the reported 0.060 is about a 5.3% reduction.
[83] (Normal) Question: Where are probabilities reliable, and does calibration hold across days and operating thresholds?
[84] (Normal) Solution: Show campaign-level reliability plots with bin counts, Brier skill against a prevalence baseline and threshold-specific precision. Keep tuning, probability calibration and risk-control calibration roles explicit. Check whether reuse of calibration labels violates the claimed procedure.
[85] (Heading 2) Conformal risk control is conditional on its assumptions
[86] (Normal) Concern: the theorem controls expected risk under its stated conditions. It does not promise that every campaign meets a target. Block dependence and campaign shift need justification. The revised threshold direction is improved, but an “83% guarantee failure” interpretation remains inappropriate without a valid test.
[87] (Normal) Question: What is the loss, exchangeable unit, threshold direction and fallback? Do all satisfy the theorem?
[88] (Normal) Solution: Specify the mathematical procedure and its assumptions before reporting a guarantee. Distinguish empirical campaign compliance from theoretical expected-risk control. An empirical 1/(n+1) correction is not a universal impossibility floor when a valid fallback exists. Reference the actual theorem [2].
[89] (Heading 2) Fallback and event coverage are different questions
[90] (Normal) Concern: the stated all-alarm fallback at threshold zero should yield zero miss loss on eligible rows under the stated rule, yet the manuscript table reports about 86% alarms and 0.018 loss in a below-floor case. No eligible alarm time also prevents detecting some events under that policy.
[91] (Normal) Question: Do the implementation, table and text use the same threshold convention, denominator and loss?
[92] (Normal) Solution: Trace the fallback on actual predictions and reconcile the numbers. Report row risk separately from event detection. An all-alarm rule over eligible rows cannot fix events excluded from all eligible windows; that requires changing the prediction policy.
[93] (Heading 2) Hawkes models uncertainty and small sample inference
[94] (Normal) Concern: an estimated branching ratio describes the fitted process; it does not prove that individual handovers caused later handovers. Residual rejection challenges fit but does not uniquely diagnose exponential decay. Four sessions offer weak support for broad uncertainty claims.
[95] (Normal) Question: Could route variation, cell layout, ping-pong or changing exposure produce the clustering?
[96] (Normal) Solution: Use descriptive clustering language and compare appropriate alternatives. Preserve session boundaries and gaps. Report per-campaign estimates and sensitivity. Explain bootstrap units and limitations rather than assuming 180 s blocks are independent. Horizon coherence is useful structure, not proof of calibration or superior forecasting; horizon aggregation can also change rankings after reweighting or calibration.

[122] (Heading 2) Event populations feature counts and interpretation
[123] (Normal) Concern: 957 raw commands, 938 retained-block commands, 10,260 retained samples and 8,586 usable rows are distinct populations. The original 28.7% of 938 return claim implied about 269 returns, exceeding the full-set count of 252 under an identical definition.
[124] (Normal) Question: Does every result identify its numerator, denominator, feature set and experiment arm?
[125] (Normal) Solution: Use a population ledger throughout. Retire stale results rather than blending them. Keep 112 primary features, ten additional signalling features and any historical feature sets clearly separate. A feature count is not evidence that all listed measurements existed.
[126] (Heading 2) Novelty external validation and causal wording
[127] (Normal) Unresolved: the literature screen includes inconsistent counts and exclusions. The public dataset description lists radio exports, whereas the thesis says only report exports are available. External six-feature signalling transfer does not validate the 112-feature main model.
[128] (Normal) Question: What exactly was screened, downloaded and tested? Which claims are descriptive, predictive or causal?
[129] (Normal) Solution: Audit the literature matrix and dataset contents [3]. Narrow novelty to the supported comparison. State external validation’s feature scope. Replace claims that a target is necessarily stronger, a return is unnecessary, an episode was declined or a performance drop is 96% causally attributable with interpretations supported by the experiment. Ablation effects are not generally additive causal contributions.
[130] (Heading 2) Recommended research claim
[131] (Normal) A defensible contribution is a signalling-grounded evaluation of handover-command forecasting from sparse measurements, with explicit timing audits, causal feature construction, campaign-level testing and measured alarm costs. Claims of precise physical advance warning, guaranteed performance under shift or prevented service failures require further evidence.
