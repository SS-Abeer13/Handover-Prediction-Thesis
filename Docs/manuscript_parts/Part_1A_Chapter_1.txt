CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

Mobility is the defining feature of a cellular network that it has compared to all other access technologies, and the handover is the mechanism providing this property. As soon as a user equipment moves outside the useful coverage area of its serving cell, the network needs to re-establish the connection to a neighbour without disrupting the service provided on the connection. This is a frequent occurrence on a dense urban network: during the measurement campaigns reported in this thesis, an average of one handover per eleven seconds of driving took place. Each of those events is a short period of time where throughput decreases, latency increases and sometimes the radio connection goes down completely.

This chapter introduces the problem that the thesis will solve, why it is a worthwhile problem to solve, and the objectives that will be used to evaluate the work. It has been described in Section 1.1 how handover is triggered in Long Term Evolution (LTE) and why the triggering rule is structurally unable to provide for an advance warning. The cost of the limitation on measured data is quantified in section 1.2. The problem is formally stated in Section 1.3, the objectives are listed in Section 1.4, a scope is bound in Section 1.5, the contributions are summarised in Section 1.6, and the organisation of the remainder of the report is described in Section 1.7.

1.1 Background

In LTE, the user equipment (UE) periodically measures the Reference Signal Received Power (RSRP) and Reference Signal Received Quality (RSRQ) of its serving cell and of the neighbour cells it is able to receive, and then reports the measurements to the network if a condition set by the network is met [1]. Event A3 is defined in 3GPP TS 36.331 as the condition when a neighbour cell becomes better than the serving cell by a configured offset and this relation holds for a configured time-to-trigger (TTT) before the report is triggered, and is the dominant condition for intra-frequency mobility [1].

There are two properties of that definition that are relevant for this thesis. The first is that the condition is expressed on a comparison which already holds: the neighbour must have improved before something happens. Second, the TTT adds another delay to quench spurious triggering due to the fast fading. Together they ensure that by the time a measurement report arrives at the handset the radio environment will have evolved. The network then takes an assessment of the report and if it determines to continue, then it sends mobilityControlInfo from the RRCConnectionReconfiguration message, mobilityControlInfo is the handover command itself.

Event A3 is thus not a prediction rule but a reaction rule and this is not a weakness of the standard. The rule can determine with certainty, it does not determine too early, and it does so with good effect. What the standard doesn't offer and what an ever-latency-more-sensitive service mix is ever more demanding for, is in advance notification: a hint that a handover is about to occur, given one or two seconds in advance, which enables the target cell to be prepared, the transmission schedule to be modified, or, an unnecessary handover to be suppressed.

1.2 Motivation

Reaction costs (not assumed from literature) were measured on the data collected for this thesis and the cost of reacting versus anticipation is measurable. A total of 938 handovers were identified from these 4 campaigns, a mean of 11 seconds and a median of 3.5 seconds between successive handovers, using signalling information to determine that a handover has taken place. Of these handovers, 24.5 % were handovers to the same cell just handed over within 15 seconds; each of these handovers correspond to 2 signalling exchanges and 2 service disconnections without any gain in new serving cell. The radio link failed and had to be reestablished 341 times. 62.9 % of the 7,385 Event A3 transmissions by the handset did not have a handover event within 2 seconds, meaning that the vast majority of the control-plane traffic generated by the rule is not leading to mobility.

Are not pathological – these are what the deployed configuration produces – for a dense urban deployment. But they do set out to show that there is something to do about a prediction horizon, even a short one. One second before a handover command is issued would be enough time to get the target-cell ready, while a warning, that a handover is likely to be reversed within 15 seconds is enough information to consider not doing it.

Naturally, one wonders if there already is such a predictor. A survey of 108 publications including an audit of 22 of these in Chapter 2 suggests it is not in a form from which an operator could act. None of those 22 models provides any mobility unit (whole drive or whole device session) when measuring a classification task upon real radio data, none provides a calibration curve, an expected calibration error or a Brier score, and none provides information about the timing of their warnings nor how many false warnings are generated per unit time. There are seven report headline accuracy for tasks with positive-class prevalence ranging from 0.86 % to 11 %, and a constant negative prediction already achieves between 89 % and 99 % accuracy.

1.3 Problem statement

It is the following problem that is addressed in this thesis. Using the information a handset has at time t — including serving and neighbour signal strengths and qualities, as well as their short-term statistics, mobility state and recent signalling history — calculate the probability that the network will send a handover command within h seconds for a number of h in parallel such that:

the estimates are mutually consistent, that is, the probability for a longer horizon is never smaller than the probability for a shorter horizon; and

the estimates are calibrated, that is, they state a probability of p and the corresponding empirical event frequency is close to p;

The evaluation protocol does not carry over information from test drive to training; and

The statement of the bound of the fraction of handovers the warning is based on the estimates will miss in future drives.

All four requirements are not met in any one place in the audited literature, and all four are not met in the handover-prediction setting.

1.4 Objectives

The thesis specific objectives are.

O1. To predict the next handover at 5 look ahead times – 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 5 seconds – based only on the quantities that are available at the handset at the instant of predicting.

O2. To set up an evaluation protocol that has no leakage between train and test, which is done by splitting all the splits at the level of the entire drive; and to determine the value of that protocol compared to the random-row splitting that is common in the comparator literature.

O3. To be useful to an operator, these probabilities must be mutually coherent across horizons, calibrated to value and have a distribution-free bound on the per-drive miss rate.

O4. To evaluate its generalisability beyond the campaign it has been developed for, the result has been tested for both a corridor and speed regime held out completely, and also on an independent public data set collected.

O5. Rather than just stating the accuracy of the prediction, to explain the mechanism leading to the prediction and therefore to characterise the behaviour of the deployed mobility configuration on this network.

1.5 Scope and limitations

It is important that the scope of this work be defined and that the limits of this definition be specified at the beginning, not at the end.

The study includes one commercial LTE operator, four measurement days, two mobility regimes and 57 drives. It does not assert that numbers results are applicable to another operator, or to another radio access technology, but there is an external validation referred to in Chapter 5 that addresses this issue. The measurement export is sampled at 1Hz, this limits the in principle possible handover event resolution to 90.5%, this is not a model limitation but instrument.

The system developed here is an offline predictor, which is tested on recorded data. It's not an integrated network and there's no assertion that putting it in place would make any network key performance indicator (KPI) better. In particular, no causal claim is made about the benefit of acting on the warning, as explained in Chapter 5: the logging policy does not have an 'off-policy' component as the propensities are zero or one. A counting upper bound is given.

Lastly, the thesis does not state anything about human health. No human subjects were used and no exposure measurements were made; the impact analysis in chapter 6 is limited to the exposure measurements.

1.6 Contributions

The following five contributions of this thesis are substantiated by the corresponding result in Chapter 5.

Discrete-Time Hazard formulation of handover prediction that provides five look-ahead horizons that are mutually consistent from a single model fit (for any learner), without a held out calibration split and no post-hoc correction. Evaluation of the measured effect: 0 % horizon-ordering violations, compared to 43.6 % for the independent per-horizon classifiers.

2. Architecture-dependent random-row splitting inflation measured on the value of grouped evaluation on drive-test radio data: 74 % for gated recurrent unit, 4 % for logistic regression, not just an increase in value.

3. A distribution-free risk guarantee and the campaign-design rule that comes from it: the number of calibration drives sets the smallest miss-rate target that can be given for a study, regardless of model quality.

4. A signalling-decoding correction (in the form of a configuration timeline for message-scoped measurement identifiers) that says that the A3 offsets deployed on this network are actually negative (not the positive values that appear in prediction literature).

5. Two characterisations of network behaviour which have not been modelled by the prediction literature: the rate of report-conversion (measured at 62.9 % took 2 seconds to decrease) and a ping-pong rate (reported concurrently with the three definitional choices that determine its value).
