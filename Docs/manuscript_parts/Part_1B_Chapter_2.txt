The literature review and methodology are presented in chapter 2.

There are four bodies of work that relate to the question posed in chapter 1 and that almost intersect but not quite. The cellular measurement literature focuses on the "what" of handovers in deployed networks, but not on "predictors". The handover-prediction literature constructs predictors; but most of the time, by simulated data and most of the time, without an evaluation protocol that would hold up to any scrutiny. The survival-analysis and pointprocess literatures provide the perfect set of mathematical objects: a selfexciting process with time-rescaling validation and a discrete-time hazard, but none have been focused on mobility data. For signal-processing tasks that have a different exchangeability structure from that found in an event stream recorded on a drive, the distribution-free uncertainty literature provides guarantees.

Each is examined in turn and then, a quantitative audit of the 22 most comparable prediction models is conducted, further revealing the research gap identified by the audit, and detailing the methodology adopted in response, as well as alternatives considered and discarded.

The Event A3 trigger is a mobility-based event that uses 2.1 LTE.

The E-UTRAN mobility procedure is defined in 3GPP TS 36.331 [1] and defined at system level in TS 36.300 [2]. The UE is configured with MeasurementObjects and ReportConfigurations, which include measurement identities that link them together; the UE checks the entry condition with the UE's filtered measurements and sends a MeasurementReport if the entry conditions have been met for the configured time-to-trigger.

The controlling event for intra-frequency mobility is Event A3. The entry condition of TS 36.331 is where Mn is the measured neighbour quantity, Ms is the serving quantity, Off is the configured offset, Ocn and Ocs are cell-individual offsets, and Hys is hysteresis.

M_n + Ocn − Hys  >  M_s + Ocs + Off	(2.1)

All the time to trigger the condition must be true before the report is sent. Then the decision to hand over is not made by the UE but by the network and is sent to the UE by an RRCConnectionReconfiguration message which includes mobilityControlInfo.

There are three implications, all of which are utilized elsewhere in this thesis. First, the report is evidence of the network's willingness to hand over – not that it will; this conversion rate from report to command is an empirical variable and is measured in Chapter 5. Second, the parameters Off, Hys and the TTT are operator programmed and cannot be seen without decoding the signalling; and the prediction literature, which assumes textbook levels, is assuming something that it has not checked. Third, the condition is defined on a state that already must be acquired, and must be maintained, so the earliest time that Event A3 can cause any output to occur is after the radio environment has changed.

2.2 The measurement literature

The networking-measurement community has a long-standing line of work related to recovering operator mobility configurations from control-plane traces on live networks. Deng et al. [3] introduce LTE precedent, a measurement study of 4G mobility configurations in operation and their impact. Ghoshal et al. [4] conduct the same analysis on a large scale on recent networks, quantifying hysteresis, threshold, offset, time-to-trigger and trigger quantity for events A1-A6, B1 and B2 per operator/per band, during over 15,000km of driving and 48,426 handovers across three operators. Hassan et al. [5] show that the use of commercial diagnostic tooling for RRC extraction is accepted methodology and Liu et al. [6] read the mobilityControlInfo for mobility analysis without recovering the numerical parameters.

This literature is of direct relevance in two respects. Methodologically, it does not directly compete with the decoding pipeline that has been used in Chapter 3, but is rather a legitimate means of doing so: it is well established that the deployed A3 parameters can be extracted from measConfig, and this thesis does not assume that this is a contribution. Substantively, it provides the reference points to interpret the configuration measured here; offsets between +6 dB and +10 dB and time-to-triggers as low as 256ms and 640ms are reported by Ghoshal et al. for the operators studied, while the network measured here operates between −15 dB and +5 dB and with a minimum time-to-trigger of 160ms.

What this literature doesn't do is predict. Ghoshal et al. make it clear that no prediction using machine learning is done, no evaluation of off-policy is performed, and no counterfactual analysis is conducted – it is descriptive study by design. The ping pong results these studies report are also descriptive, and—as Section 2.4 shows—under definitions different enough to double the number.

The prediction literature and protocol audit are used to determine the quality of the teaching.

Many works have been proposed that use learned models to predict handover, radio-link failure or next-cell occupancy, in a large body of work. Its characterisation from the outside is a recent systematic review which characterizes it unfavourably. Ankome and Hanada [7] reviewed 429 reports, deduplicated to 336 and included 49 studies from 2010 to 2025, but were unable to perform a meta analysis due to too great a diversity of simulators and outcome definitions across the included studies, they noted that the majority of studies report percentage improvements over an independently chosen baseline rather than actual performance, and they noticed that data splitting, class imbalance and calibration were rarely explicitly discussed throughout the included set of reports. Saoud et al. [8] conclude that there is no quantitative cross comparison between the different strategies in the field and Chabira et al. [9] list the metric vocabulary used in the field, which does not include any prevalence-aware ranking metric or calibration.

Of all those publications screened for this thesis, 108 make a prediction of handover, or a prediction of a 'next-cell' or 'radio-link' failure and were judged on protocol quality rather than on a headline performance. The audit is reported in Table 2.1.

Two of the observations of the audit bear upon the rest of this thesis.

The first relates to metrics. There are 22 report accuracy figures between 0.86 % and 11 % (at which a constant negative prediction scores between 89 % and 99 %) and seven of these are 98.03 %, 99.84 % and 94.83 %. Another similar failure is in a railway radio-link-failure study, which reports an AUC > 0.95 in all six of the architectures it compares, in a task with roughly one positive out of every five-hundred samples, and hence fails to distinguish between any of the architectures.

The second relates to splitting and the exceptions are not rounded away. Of these 22 do, 5 do so in a spatial zone, by device, by time under a rolling-origin protocol on operator data, by travel day [10] and by deployment event [11]. But none of the five classifies a mobility unit for measured radio for a per timestep classification task, and the two newest additions are regression tasks that do not involve prevalence; the best protocol in the set was for next-day cell-level radio-link failure, not for a per-sample mobility forecast.

The pattern can be attributed to a structural aspect of this literature. It is anti-correlated between protocol quality and measurement quality. The studies that are least clear about the split protocol are the ones with the data most similar to that used here – namely, the studies with the cleanest splits operate on ray-traced or SUMO-simulated trajectories [10] or on aggregated operator key performance indicators, or at cell-and-day level [11]. Recent graph-based models have shown both sides: TH-GCN [12] creates a dynamic graph of the UE and the cells in dense vehicular settings, and it provides simulated results, does not specify any split protocol, and reports relative (to an arbitrary baseline) changes in important KPIs; GRIMCELL [11] uses graph learning on data for radio-planning, cell configuration management and performance management from a commercial LTE-A Pro network across 55 real deployment events, but it predicts KPIs per cell per day, so it is more like a planning tool than a per-second warning system. Graser et al. [13] categorise the trajectory-learning literature according to data density, and note that the dense individual-trace regime, in which one-hertz drive data falls, is the least-studied regime for event prediction, compared to location prediction.

The narrow statement that the audit can support, and this thesis can support, is therefore the following: of the 22 models audited, 10 state they don't split at all, five split on a unit coarser than the row, and none at all reports a split on the mobility unit for a per-timestep classification task on measured radio data; none reports calibration; and none reports how early warnings arrive.

2.4 Ping pong and its definitional instability

There is a wide spread reporting of handover ping-pong, which is inconsistently defined. No standardised definition in 3GPP; only a return in a window (author decides), which changes the reported rate a lot. The ping-pong measurements have been reported on real 4G networks by Zidic et al. [14] and per-operator rates of 15 % to 25 % have been reported under a window of fifteen seconds and a denominator of per handover by Ghoshal et al. [4].

There are 3 choices that determine the number and papers seldom give all three: if a cell is identified based on the physical cell identity, or the physical cell identity plus the carrier; if any return within a window counts or if only returns to the previous cell are counted; and if the denominator is the number of raw handover events or number of quality controlled handover events. The effects are so profound that they debunk the notion of cross-paper comparison: Amirova et al. [15] report a ping-pong rate of 0.13 % (which is based on the division by measurement records and not handovers). The complete hierarchy of these options is reported on a set of 938 handovers, all of which were identical in Chapter 5, with the rate varying from 24.5 % to 41.3 % due to the change of options, but not of the data.

2.5 Appropriate formulation - Survival analysis

The problem defined in Section 1.3 — likelyhood of occurrence within h seconds for a number of h values — is actually a time-to-event problem and as such the adequate formulation is not a set of independent classifiers but a survival model. In fact, Wiegrebe et al. [16] survey 61 methods in deep time-to-event, and conclude that the discrete-time route (where the time axis is binned and binary indicators per time interval trained as classification) is solidly in the mainstream. Thorsen-Meyer et al. [17] build up the structure in the same way for prediction of patient outcomes in intensive care, where they use a censoring-aware log-likelihood over discretised windows.

This literature is used here without an intention to novelty the formulation per se and the fact that it is so stated removes an obvious reviewer objection. The novelty is the direction in which the formulation is directed: no model in the 22-paper audit of Section 2.3 presents handover prediction like this, and each therefore has the incoherence mentioned in Section 4.2.

There is one exception to the survival literature – it is deliberate. That literature predominantly assesses with the concordance index and the integrated Brier score that both aggregate over the horizon axis. However, an operator works at one horizon. However, the model with the good average rank, and claiming that the probability of handover in one second is greater than the probability of handover in three seconds on 43.6 % of the samples, cannot be deployed, regardless of its concordance index. Chapter 4 thus takes a horizon-wise approach and reports AUPRC horizon-wise and also a coherence-violation rate.

2.6 Distribution-free uncertainty quantification

The conformal prediction approach gives finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees under the assumption of exchangeability and this approach has already been exploited within wireless communications by a strong group. All of these approaches are naturally generalised to a deployment lifecycle, including pre-deployment conformal calibration and hyperparameter selection, monitoring during distribution shift, and post-deployment counterfactual analysis, as done by Cohen, Park, Simeone and Shamai [18] for demodulation, modulation classification and channel prediction. The conformal risk control is due to Angelopoulos et al. [20]: it is an extension of the guarantee from a prediction set to the expectation of a bounded monotone risk.

Nevertheless, the assertion made in this thesis is somewhat more circumscribed than it may seem and the restriction is intentional. The guarantees in [18] and [19] are over prediction sets, for independent and identically distributed tasks in a frame. The guarantee in Chapter 4 is for a risk (namely, the operator key performance indicator, the per-drive miss rate), and for a stream that is exchangeable only at the level of a whole drive. The identification and defence of that exchangeability unit is the contribution; the campaign-design floor that follows from it is its consequence; and no wireless conformal study located in three screening passes reports a mobility-management case at all.

The self-excitation of handover arrivals is considered in 2.7 Point processes.

Events of handover are not independent of one another. The standard model for arrival streams in which each arrival increases the intensity of future arrivals is the self-exciting point process introduced by Hawkes [21] and treated in the contemporary reference by Laub et al. [22] which was used in Chapter 5 to give the branching-ratio interpretation. Installing such a process does not necessarily imply that the process is self-exciting, the fit needs to be tested. The standard check is the residual analysis of Ogata [23] whereby the time axis is rescaled using the fitted compensator and the time rescaled arrivals are checked against a unit-rate Poisson process; in a networking context, Price-Williams and Heard [24] used the same technique to validate a self-exciting fit to campus and laboratory network traffic by time rescaling the arrivals and testing against a unit-rate Poisson process. In keeping with that tradition, Chapter 5 presents that test result, and, in particular, where it fails the fitted kernel.

2.8 Evaluation practice from neighbouring fields

Two additional findings from nearby literatures are mentioned here because they make sense of the negative findings in Chapter 5 and thus do not cause embarrassment. Ismail Fawaz et al. [25] compare the performance of nine unsupervised domain adaptation algorithms on twelve time series datasets, and conclude that several published algorithms do worse than no adaptation but using the source only, and that the adaptation method is more important than the backbone itself; this explains why Chapter 5 achieves a null result for Deep CORAL [26] and does not indicate that there is anything wrong with the implementation of this method. Shi et al. [27] provide the shift taxonomy, which would refer to the type of shift presented in this case as a device-and-day shift, not a sensor modality shift. The commonly-used metrics for time-series event detection each satisfy only a subset of the desirable properties and none satisfy all of these properties, which is why Wagner et al. [28] report the event-level detection rate, lead time and false alarms per hour along with the row-level AUPRC.

Lastly the standards bear on the selection of the horizon. Deb et al. [29] model the conditional handover as a discrete time Markov chain (DTMC) on preparation/execution offsets and timers and make it explicit that conditional handover takes a lead time, not a label: a model which can identify the correct target cell two hundred milliseconds before the event doesn't buy anything, because the preparation message must be sent, acknowledged, and stored. This is the analytical rationale for reasons to report a lead-time distribution and retain the time horizons of two, three, and five seconds even though the trigger used in the deployment fires after a few hundred milliseconds.

2.9 Research gap

Having collected the above sections, the space which this thesis is to fill can now be exactly defined. Ground truth data to measurement level is available, but is not connected to prediction. There are models to make predictions, but not under an evaluation protocol with a holding out mobility unit and calibrated outputs, and not with event level costs. The correct formulation is present in the literature on survival but not in literature related to mobility events. Although there are distribution-free guarantees in wireless, they are for a different exchangeability structure, and not for any mobility-management task.

This work fills in that gap: measurement-quality ground truth derived from decoding the signalling, borrowed formulation made coherent across horizons, exchangeability unit stated and justified through a conformal risk bound, and evaluation protocol that is acceptable to a reviewer from the machine-learning community, not just one from the wireless community.

2.10 Methodology

The methodology adopted is an outcome of the gap. It can be understood as a five step process as shown in Figure 2.1 and each step is explained in detail in Chapters 3 and 4.

Stage 1 — Collection. Four drive-test campaigns are performed with an instrumented handset, with a 1Hz radio measurement export, and concurrently a decoded RRC signalling log, performed on a live commercial LTE network.

Stage 2 – Ground truth and configuration recovery. Handover instants are derived from RRCConnectionReconfiguration messages which contain mobilityControlInfo that are received and successfully decoded. Every measurement report is replayed against the configuration from the time of the measurement because measurement identifiers are scoped to the measurement in which they are carried.

Stage 3 — Feature construction. 100% of the candidate features are strictly causal (closed at the prediction instant) and no candidate feature crosses a drive boundary, with 152 candidate features constructed from radio, mobility, history and signalling sources.

Stage 4 - Formulation and modelling. The problem is stated in terms of survival in discrete time. Under the product-limit identity, the per-interval hazard and horizon probabilities are recovered from a single model, while ensuring that they are ordered. There are 7 learners that are compared under the same conditions.

Stage 5 – Evaluation and risk control. Splits are bucketed by whole drive, and induction is carried out over 5 seeds, while the certified warning threshold is via conformal risk control, with the exchangeable unit being a drive.

This section discusses approaches that were considered by the Committee and rejected.

Four alternatives were seriously considered and rejected and the reasons are recorded here as part of the methodological argument.

Independent per-horizon classifiers. The straightforward solution (one binary classifier for each look-ahead time) was used and kept as a control arm, but not used as the main solution. It is dismissed as the preferred approach since it itself generates conflicting predictions on a large proportion of samples, as Chapter 5 measures, and for the cost of a held-out split and the lost prediction power.

Random-row cross-validation. The majority of comparator literature following the splitting protocol is done only to measure what it is worth. It is turned down due to the fact that two one-second samples that are next to each other in a drive will be very similar, and a random split puts almost the same row on either side of the split, causing inflation, as shown in Chapter 5, that depends on the architecture, and thus changes the ranking of models.

Imbalance reweighting of classes. The usual reflex to a rare-event task is to reweight the positive class. This is because reweighting transforms the fitted probability monotonically (so that ranking metrics are unchanged but the probability scale is destroyed) and the product-limit identity multiplies K such probabilities together, which means that a multiplicative error per interval gets compounded with the horizon. Both arms are thus fitted unweighted and it is also a fair comparison.

Learning handover decision by reinforcement learning. A decision-theoretic formulation, where the agent learns when to hand over was considered and chosen by the closest comparator study found on this network. It is denied here because it is an answer to a different question: It optimises a decision the network is allowed to make at its own speed, but this thesis predicts an event. For completeness, a re-implementation of that comparator scored as a predictor is reported in chapter 5.
