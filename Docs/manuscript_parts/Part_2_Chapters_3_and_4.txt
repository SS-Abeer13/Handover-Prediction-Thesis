In this chapter, the measurement campaign and the ground truth are described.

All the claims in this thesis are based on the quality of the measurement underpinning them and this chapter describes how this measurement was achieved. The instrumentation is described in Section 3.1. The four campaigns and the routes are described in Section 3.2. The signalling-decoding procedure is described in section 3.3 including the required configuration-timeline correction. How continuous driving is segmented into drives and quality controlled is described in section 3.4. The mobility configuration deployed as a result of the signalling is reported in Section 3.5, and the dataset summarised in Section 3.6.

3.1 Instrumentation

Measurements were taken using a commercial drive-test handset that runs Accuver XCAL and outputs a log of the radio-measurement results sampled at one hertz and a control-plane log containing the RRC messages sent by the handset and the network into the drive-test handset. This class of instrumentation has been used for RRC extraction in the measurement literature [5], [4] in the context of live networks.

The radio export includes the serving-cell physical cell identity (PCI), E-UTRA absolute radio frequency channel number (EARFCN), RSRP, RSRQ and signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) for the serving cell, and for each neighbour cell detected, in addition to a Global Positioning System (GPS) fix with speed and heading. The signallog contains full RRCConnectionReconfiguration, MeasurementReport and RRCConnectionReestablishment message sequence with millisecond timings.

The signalling log is a design decision and is the basis for the ground truth throughout. A counter that indicates that the vendor's internal logic determined that a handover occurred; the handover command is a decoded RRCConnectionReconfiguration with mobilityControlInfo; the value of the counter is set to the time when the vendor receives the command. The difference is significant for a prediction task, since the label instant indicates what it means to be "one second ahead.

3.2 The four campaigns

In September 2026, four campaigns have been carried out on a single commercial LTE operator, in two different mobility regimes. The three covers are urban corridors - an urban arterial road, an urban loop and a dense urban area in Dhaka. The fourth is the Uttara-Gazipur highway which has a mean speed of 49.5 km/h, about three times of the mean urban speed. The routes and geographical distribution of handover events recorded on the routes are shown in figure 3.1.

The first hint that the prediction signal is in part geometric is the clustering seen in Figure 3.1 and it is also the first counter argument of the random-row splitting: If handovers point to recognisable locations, then the signal can be learned from the training rows and detected from the test rows in the same drive by a random-row splitting.

The fourth campaign receives a special treatment in the design of the experiment and is treated accordingly throughout the experiment. It was driven after all modelling choices were made: the feature set, the formulation, the model family, the hyperparameters, the evaluation protocol were all determined beforehand and documented prior to the existence of the highway data. It is thus not an extra fold, but is a true out-of-sample test of a new corridor and a new speed regime as reported in Chapter 5.

Signalling decoding and configuration timeline

3.3.1 The attribution chain

To resolve a measurement report to the event that triggered it, requires a chain of three objects defined in TS 36.331 [1]. The measId is carried in a MeasurementReport. The measIdToAddModList associates a measurement object — that defines the frequency of the carrier being measured — with a report configuration — that defines the trigger event and the trigger parameters. To determine if a given report is an A3 report, and to determine under which offset and time to trigger it fired, that chain must be followed.

3.3.2 Why a flat parse is incorrect

The problem is that the configuration is not sent as an object. It is supplied in fragments over a connection's lifetime in multiple RRCConnectionReconfiguration messages, which add, update and remove entries, and the identifiers are message specific. Different bindings of measurement-object-to-report-configuration can have the same measId at different points in the same connection.

There is no way for a parser to flatten the log — to gather all the configuration pieces together and to resolve all the reports against the total configuration — to result in anything but an imprecise answer. When applied to this dataset, this parse marked 99.4 % of all measurement reports as Event A3, including measurement reports sent when no neighbour cell was reported in the measurement export at all. The report for the Event A3 was based on a neighbour quantity and therefore could not be correct if there were no neighbours and it was this peculiarity of an impossible consequence that revealed the error, not that there was a difference in one of the metrics.

3.3.3 The timeline

The correction is to re-play the configuration in the form of time line. Each fragment of configuration is time-stamped, and applied sequence-first; this results, for every time t, in exactly a set of bindings of measId in effect at t. Each MeasurementReport is then resolved at the time of the MeasurementReport's timestamp, in relation to the state of that timeline on that date. The difference between the two procedures is shown in figure 3.2.

The percentage of measurement reports that resolve to Event A3 is between 43 % and 52 % of all reports, depending on the campaign, and the remaining 20 % or so are split between A1, A2 and A5. All quantities reported in this thesis, which are report dependent, in particular the report-conversion rate of Section 5.8, are obtained after the correction and no pre-corrected quantity is ever quoted.

3.4 Segmentation and quality control

The continuous logging delivers one long record for each campaign, with stationary portions and intervals of signal acquisition transients and sometimes gaps where the tool was restarted. A drive is a continuous piece of a recording that meets the following requirements: the sample grid must not be interrupted, there must be a valid serving-cell attachment, and the recording must run for at least sixty seconds. The 60 s minimum is required for the feature construction of Section 4.5 (rolling windows of up to ten seconds) and the evaluation must include a sufficient number of independent measurements for each drive.

The raw handover count is 957 and this is reduced to 938 after segmentation & quality control. There are 19 events that are not in a qualifying drive (acquisition transients or segments less than sixty seconds in duration or gaps in the sample grid).

Both counts are reported here because both are correct in their definitions; the difference is explained when a handover count appears in this thesis. All of the models presented in Chapter 5 are fit and validated on the 938 events in the quality-controlled drives. The 957 figure is only applied where the raw event stream is the appropriate object: namely, the ungrouped variant of the ping-pong definition ladder in Section 5.9.

3.5 The deployed configuration

There are four campaigns that can be replayed to restore the operator's deployed intra-frequency mobility parameters. Table 3.1 reports them.

There are two points about Table 3.1 to be commented upon. First, the configuration is fixed to be the same in all four campaigns, and that is what makes it a leave one campaign out experiment in Section 5.6, a test of corridor and speed not a test of the configuration. Second, most of the offsets are negative. The A3 offset is negative, meaning it means that the measurement report is triggered when the neighbour is still below the serving cell by the amount of the A3 offset; this is much more aggressive than the +6 to +10 dB offsets reported for the operators measured by Ghoshal et al. [4] and is opposite to the +3 dB offset assumed as a textbook default in several of the prediction studies audited in Section 2.3.

It's a small discovery that would have a huge impact on literature. A prediction study that assumes a positive offset is taking the position that a handover will only take place after the neighbour has become better; on this network this is not the case for most handovers, and features and baselines based on such an assumption are mis-specified.

3.6 The dataset

The pooled data spans about 95 km of driving. On average, handover takes place every eleven seconds with the median inter-handover gap being three and a half seconds. The signalling log also contains 341 RRCConnectionReestablishment procedures, which are times the signalling connection was not handed over but lost and had to be re-established; it is these that are motivating for Section 1.2. The distribution of samples and events over the campaigns is shown in figure 3.4.

There are three properties of this dataset that limit what can be concluded from it; these are not included in the conclusion but are included here.

Its data volume is small for machine learning, but big for drive tests. There are 57 independent groups, enough for a grouped evaluation with bootstrap intervals, as required by the protocol but not enough for training a sequence model from scratch, as Section 5.2 demonstrates.

Event resolution is limited by the sampling rate. If the preceding second is empty, a handover with one sample per second can't be predicted at all. The percentage of events that can, in principle, be resolved on this grid is 90.5 %, so that all the figures given in Chapter 5 for event-level detections are reported with reference to this, and not to 100 %.

The data set consists of one operator and four days. Table 3.1 is the configuration of one operator, the cross-regime transfer described in Section 5.6 thus relies on a single configuration pair; and the external validation of Section 5.7 is to some extent a reflection of the fact that this limitation was designed for rather than retrofitted on.

4.1 Problem formalisation

Suppose there is a drive d with samples indexed by t on a uniform one-second grid. At every sample, the handset watches a feature vector xt, which is formed from measurements it has at or before the present sample t. Let Tt be the time until the next handover command on the same drive.

To estimate, for a given set of levels h1 &lt; h2 &lt; … &lt; hK, the total occurrence.

F_k(x_t) = P( T_t ≤ h_k │ x_t ),   k = 1 … K	(4.1)

with K = 5 and horizons of 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 5 seconds. Samples for which the drive is terminated before the next handover are right-censored: it is known that this happens only after the end of the drive, but not when. Here right censoring is not to be discarded, but rather the last samples of each drive are censored, and discarding them would waste data and would bias the resulting observed event time distribution towards the lower end.

4.3 Binary formulation with multiple horizons and its defect

Motivation. The most straightforward way to tackle Equation (4.1) is to create a binary label yt,k = 1{ Tt ≤ hk } for each horizon and train K classifiers. It is the formulation that is implicitly assumed in each of the models in the audit of Section 2.3 and that does not need any special machines.

The defect. Each of the K classifiers is trained separately and can thus make predictions that do not necessarily satisfy the ordering requirement of its own definition. Since { Tt ≤ h1 } is a subset of { Tt ≤ h2 } whenever h1 &lt; h2, it is required that F1 ≤ F2 ≤ ... ≤ FK at each sample. Independent fits do not enforce this, and it is measured in Section 5.4 how many times the independent fit violates this, which is 43.6 % of the samples.

Violation is not a minor numerical irregularity! It makes the utterly absurd claim that a handover is more likely in one second than three and it ruins the notion of the output being a probability. If an operator is given such a pair of numbers, he/she cannot do anything with either of them.

The problems of post-hoc repair. The defect can be corrected ex-post by projecting the K outputs onto the monotone cone, or by calibrating each horizon individually by isotonic regression. Both the repairs were carried out and both are reported as controls in Section 5.4. For two reasons: both require a held-out split that the data set cannot spare, and per-horizon isotonic calibration measurably harms coherence.

4.3 The discrete-time survival formulation

Motivation. The ordering constraint follows from the definition of the cumulative incidence, therefore a natural way to do this is to estimate a quantity from which ordering will follow rather than to estimate the ordered quantities and repair them.

Design. Divide the time axis into K bins with the highest edge being exactly the horizon of interest:

B_1 = (0, h_1],  B_2 = (h_1, h_2],  …,  B_K = (h_{K−1}, h_K]

and the hazard function in the discrete time case is defined as the probability of occurrence in bin k conditional on not having occurred at the beginning of the bin:

λ_k(x) = P( T ∈ B_k │ T > h_{k−1}, x )	(4.2)

The survivals function is the product of the per-bin survivals,

S_k(x) = Π_{j=1..k} ( 1 − λ_j(x) ) = P( T > h_k │ x )	(4.3)

Its complement is the cumulative incidence that is needed in Equation (4.1) and is known as the product-limit identity:

F_k(x) = P( T ≤ h_k │ x ) = 1 − Π_{j=1..k} ( 1 − λ_j(x) )	(4.4)

Technical advantage. Monotonicity is no longer a promise, but a fact. Since λj ∈ [0,1], every factor (1 − λj) lies in [0,1], so

S_{k+1} = S_k · ( 1 − λ_{k+1} ) ≤ S_k   ⟹   F_{k+1} ≥ F_k	(4.5)

The argument does not require any of the properties of the learner not included in its range of values. Any model that gives a number in the interval [0,1] per bin results in a consistent set of horizon probabilities in Equation (4.4). It is the one most important structural property of the system developed here, and this is why the coherence result reported in Section 5.4 is not learner independent, but the calibration result is.

4.4 Probabilities under censoring

Suppose that the event happens in the bin kt for the sample t and let ct be the last bin completely survived before censoring. Let Rt = {1 … min(kt, ct)} be the set of bins that the sample actually went into, and zt,k = 1{ k = kt } be the indicator of whether the sample went into that bin. The discrete-time survival log-likelihood can then be written in one expression that includes both the uncensored and censored cases:

ℓ = Σ_t Σ_{k ∈ R_t} [ z_{t,k} log λ_k(x_t) + (1 − z_{t,k}) log(1 − λ_k(x_t)) ]	(4.6)

The binary cross-entropy in (4.6) is the exactly one over the extended set of (sample × at-risk bin) pairs. The practical implication is that fitting a practical discrete-time survival model requires no specialised software: The data can be transformed from wide to long format, and the model can learn the shape of the baseline hazard by adding the bin index k as a feature and fitting one binary classifier. Note that a censored sample does not get thrown away, but accounts for a (1 − λk) factor for each bin that it survived.

The expansion on this data set is from 10,260 rows to about 34,000 (sample, bin) pairs. The per-bin hazard rates measured across folds are given in Table 4.2.

4.5 Non-admissibility of class reweighting

The typical approach to a rare-event task is to reweight the positive class (e.g., using the scale_pos_weight parameter of a gradient-boosting implementation). That response cannot be used in the hazard arm (specifically for Equation (4.4))

Multiplying the positive class by w has a monotonic effect on the fitted probability,

λ̃ = w λ / ( w λ + (1 − λ) )	(4.7)

This transformation doesn't change the ranking metrics, as AUPRC and AUROC only rely on the ranking of scores. The probability scale is broken, though, and Equation (4.4) is a product of K of those broken probability scales. Hence, there is a per-bin multiplicative calibration error ε which compounds along the horizon axis.

F̂_k / F_k ~ ( 1 + ε )^k	(4.8)

so the distortion will increase as the horizon is reached, just where calibration is already weakest. Both the hazard arm and the independent-classifier control arm are therefore fitted unweighted to the additional benefit of a fair comparison between the two.

4.6 Feature construction

Motivation. The system must be able to predict from what quantities a handset really has at the moment of prediction. The following two constraints apply: No feature can access information from the future and No feature can access information from a different drive.

Design. A degenerate-feature filter is used, which discards columns of zero variance or with more than 50 % missing values on the training rows, resulting in 107 columns being kept. These are divided in four blocks, summarised in Table 4.3.

Each rolling window is forward exclusive, meaning that each window closes at the prediction instant t, and computation of each window is per drive, i.e., no window goes beyond a drive boundary. A scaler fit on the combined data set would be a leak in itself, and whilst not a large leak, is one of the first things a careful reviewer will look at when reviewing your code.

An explicit leakage guard is needed for the signalling block. A measurement report is a causal antecedent of the handover it triggers, and it is in the log almost simultaneous with it. A foreseeable 'time since last A3 report' feature thus provides information on the very handover that is being predicted. The guard at the application restricts the reports that can be referenced in a signalling feature, evaluated at t, for horizon h to reports that have been received at or before t. The results of the removal of the guard are reported in section 5.11.

4.7 Models compared

A comparison of 7 learners under identical folds, identical seeds and the identical hazard formulation. The comparison is constructed in such a way that the only difference between arms is the learner.

The Event A3 rule. The deployed rule is scored as if it were a predictor, against the recovered configuration of Table 3.1 at the times that the entry condition of Equation (2.1) is satisfied. As mentioned, this is the most crucial baseline to the thesis as it is the current one operating in the network.

Logistic regression. A regularised linear model with the 107 features, which is the simplest learner to use all features.

Gradient-boosted trees. The main model is LightGBM [30]. It is selected for a tabular task with a heterogeneous (mixed scale) feature set, in which splits parallel to the axes are a good match for the data, and for which missing-value handling is a native capability.

Multi-layer perceptron. A feed-forward network on the same tabular features, provided to dissociate the contribution of non-linearity from the temporal memory.

Sequence models. All three of them are temporal convolutional network (TCN), Transformer encoder and a gated recurrent unit (GRU) that take a window of raw per-second measurements instead of the engineered summary features. These arms assess the performance of a model that learns its own temporal representation as compared to a model provided hand-built rolling statistics.

The number of trials is twenty per arm per neural network; the number of trials is equal for the tree arm and the linear arm. The result is reported in section 5.2 – no arm with temporal memory can outperform the tabular arms on this size of dataset.

4.8 Calibration

In Section 1.3 the ranking quality cannot be used for the task of determining the asserted probability as a representative value for an empirical frequency. Three calibration treatments are tested.

Temperature scaling [31] learns a single scalar on a held-out partition, which will be multiplied by the logits (before sigmoid). It is the least expressive treatment, and cannot change the ranking.

An isotonic regression [32] is a step function with a non-decreasing shape per horizon. More expressive; can fix a larger class of miscalibration; uses up a held-out split; is free to reorder horizons relative to one another when applied per horizon.

An incoherent set of horizon outputs is repaired by the monotone projection. It is only applied to the control arm because it is not required by the hazard formulation.

The quality of the calibration is given as the expected error of calibration, that is, the weighted mean absolute distance between the confidence and the empirical frequencies in equal-mass intervals, and the Brier score is also presented.

4.9 Evaluation protocol

Motivation. The protocol is the part that is most likely to make a result from this kind of data invalid, since neighbouring one-second samples within a drive are almost identical copies of each other. The consequence is measured in Section 5.3.

Design. There are five parts to the evaluation protocol.

Grouping. Splitting is done per whole drive. A drive occurs on either training or test on a fold, but not in both. The rotation is shown in figure 4.2.

Rotation. Over the 57 drives, a four-fold rotation is performed, meaning that each drive is tested exactly once per seed and is approximately 14 drives held out on each fold for training.

Repetition. This rotation is repeated with five random seeds and twenty paired observations for each comparison are thus obtained. It's not because people like the way it looks. A paired test over four observations can only achieve a p-value of < 0.125 with four folds, irrespective of how big the effect is, so the test result would represent the resolution of the test, not a property of the data.

Interval estimation. Confidence intervals are computed using a cluster bootstrap; whole drives are resampled with replacement as samples within a drive are not independent.

Fold hygiene. The feature scaler, the decision threshold and the probability calibrator are trained within the training partition of each fold only.

Metrics. There are four families reported. The ranking quality is reported as AUPRC with the prevalence floor and the lift over the floor, as well as AUROC. Reported calibration is via expected error in calibration and Brier score. Coherence is indicated as a fraction of the number of samples where the horizon ordering is not upheld. Event-level cost is indicated in the percentage of handover events delivered, the number of false alarms per hour and per kilometre and the lead time distribution. The reporting of all four families is deliberate because, as Wagner et al. [28] demonstrate, no single aggregate metric for event detection can satisfy all of the desirable properties, and thus the event-level metrics are reported in addition to the row-level metrics rather than as a replacement.

4.10 Distribution-free risk control

Motivation. A probability isn't an operating point yet. To turn the model output into a warning, a threshold must be set; an operator has the right to know what the threshold provides on drives he or she never experienced.

Design. Let the alarm thresholds λ be indexed by λ ∈ Λ where the index set is ordered in decreasing severity of the alarm, and let R(λ) be a bounded non-increasing risk. According to conformal risk control [20] the selection is made of

λ̂ = inf { λ : ( n·R̂(λ) + B ) / ( n + 1 ) ≤ α }	(4.9)

Here R̂(λ) is the empirical risk over n exchangeable calibration units and B is an upper bound on the loss, B = 1. The guarantee is that for a new exchangeable unit, Rn+1(λ̂) ≤ α, distribution-free, without assuming anything about the model, or the data-generating process, except exchangeability.

Exchangeable unit is the drive. It is this option that gives the guarantee some meaning, and this is the contribution of this section. Samples from the same drive are not interchangeable with samples from another drive. The cell sequence, traffic condition and trajectory are common to all the samples from the same drive. The risk is thus set to the miss rate per drive.

R_d(λ) = 	{ t ∈ d : y_t = 1 ∧ p̂_t < λ }

and R̂(λ) is averaged on calibration drives and not over samples.

Technical advantage, and a design rule that flows from this. If it is not possible to achieve α on the left-hand side of (4.9) then the equation cannot be satisfied. The smallest value that can be attained is B/(n+1), for threshold R̂ = 0, the most permissive, the feasibility floor is

α ≥ 1/(n+1)   ⟺   n ≥ 1/α − 1	(4.11)

The Equation (4.11) is a rule of thumb for campaign design: 19 calibration drives are needed for a study promising a 5 % miss rate, and 49 for a study promising a 2 % miss rate, regardless of the quality of the model. With a pooled calibration, across the four campaigns here, n = 28 and thus α ≥ 0.034. It is a rule that is not mentioned in the handover literature and it is by how much this is applied to driving that the guarantee is even expressible in a study.

4.12 Point-process analysis of point process departures

Motivation. The ping-pong behaviour (Section 5.9) indicates that handover events are not independent. Clustering can only be quantified by a model of the arrival process itself, not of the per-sample probability.

Design. The arrival process of the handover events on each drive is modelled as a self-exciting Hawkes process [21] [22] whose conditional intensity

μ(t) = μ_0 + Σ_{t_i < t} α · exp( −β ( t − t_i ) )	(4.12)

where every arrival increases the intensity of the subsequent arrivals by α, and the excitation decays at rate β. The expected number of direct descendants per event is called the branching ratio, α/β, and is a summary of the strength of the clustering.

Validation. The fit alone does not constitute self-excitation! The validation of the fit is done, following the networking tradition of Price-Williams and Heard [24] by a residual analysis as described by Ogata [23]: the time axis is rescaled by the fitted compensator, and the transformed inter-arrival times tested against a unit-rate exponential distribution with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, applied to held-out drives. This branching ratio, plus the result of that test (whether it passes or fails the fitted kernel), is reported in Section 5.10.
