\chapter*{ABSTRACT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Long Term Evolution networks execute handovers through Event A3, a rule that fires only after a neighbour cell has already become stronger than the serving cell by a configured offset and has remained stronger for a full time-to-trigger. The rule is reactive by construction: it issues a response, not a warning. This thesis asks whether the next handover can be forecast from the measurements a handset already reports, how far ahead, with what guarantee --- and, as it turned out, what has to be verified before any such number can be believed.

Four drive-test campaigns were conducted on a live commercial LTE network in Dhaka and Gazipur, yielding 4 continuous logging sessions, 10,260 samples at one hertz and 957 handover commands decoded from RRC signalling (yielding 938 quality-controlled commands inside retained session blocks, and 934--937 horizon-evaluable events under lead-window boundaries). An alignment audit of the one-hertz export is reported first, because it governs every result that follows: in 76\% of handovers the export row stamped before the handover command already carries the post-handover serving cell, so a row summarises the second that follows its own timestamp. Every feature is therefore lagged by one row, which moves the one-second AUPRC of the same fixed-parameter model from 0.604 to 0.156; an arm-wise decomposition attributes 94\% of that fall to the serving-cell radio scalars (where leak elimination and one-second feature ageing are confounded) and negligible impact to lagging kinematics. The defect is consistent with an end-of-second aggregation mechanism: a row written at the end of its second carries the target cell whenever the handover completes before that second closes, an empirical hypothesis consistent with an in-sample diagnostic check on the 15 handovers here whose execution crosses the boundary (0/15; 95\% Wilson score interval [0.0\%, 20.4\%], while the 211 residual cases are consistent with an additional 1-row exporter refresh delay). Applying an end-of-second rule to native-rate data is sufficient to reproduce the defect on 4,712 handovers from an external multi-carrier XCAL corpus spanning 3 operators \cite{ref40} (93\% contaminated under an end-of-second rule, 4\% under a start-of-second rule on the same events), and the defect is absent from a public log whose rows are instantaneous samples \cite{ref37}; whether the original exporter applies exactly that rule remains inferred until these captures are re-exported at native rate. The position of the contamination cliff, 50--75 ms before the second closes, is likewise an inferred logging characteristic: sampling phase, timestamp rounding, diagnostic buffering and exporter commit behaviour have not been separated. The task is posed as discrete-time survival: one model estimates per-interval hazards and horizon probabilities are products of hazards, so ordering holds by construction for any learner.

Under leave-one-campaign-out evaluation --- the protocol whose units represent distinct physical sessions here --- gradient-boosted trees reach pooled AUPRC 0.169 at one second across the four campaigns (observed range over the four held-out campaigns [0.125, 0.223], which is not a prediction interval for a new campaign; within-session 180~s block-bootstrap 95\% interval [0.150, 0.196], which is anti-conservative) against a 6.8\% prevalence floor (2.5x lift, AUROC 0.773, median lead time 0.50~s) and AUPRC 0.454 at five seconds (1.7x, campaign spread [0.385, 0.531]), against 0.114 for the implemented A3 signal-margin proxy scored as a predictor. Splitting rows at random instead inflates the same number by 49 \%, and splitting by 180-second chunks of one session --- the protocol this work used before the audit --- still inflates it by 12 \%. The hazard formulation yields zero ordering violations against 38.9\% of rows for independent per-horizon classifiers, a defect that a cumulative-maximum projection also removes at no cost, so the formulation is defended on needing neither a held-out calibration split nor five separate fits rather than on coherence alone. Scored as a warning rather than as a ranking, the system detects 33\% of handover commands at the one-second horizon at 169 false alarms per hour with a median lead of 0.50 s, and 44\% at five seconds at 77 per hour with a median lead of 3.36 s; no operating point measured here is deployable for target-cell preparation, which would also require identifying the target cell and a lead time long enough to complete preparation, and the contribution is the measurement framework rather than a deployable predictor. Conformal risk control is investigated as an empirical calibration diagnostic rather than an operating guarantee: its finite-sample guarantee assumes exchangeable calibration and test units, which campaigns separated by macro-environmental covariate shift are not (and the 12 rotations are pseudoreplicates of $n=4$ physical sessions), and 35.8\% of handover commands lack an eligible prediction row at 1~s under the chosen 2~s post-handover blanking policy. As an empirical calibration diagnostic, the pooled mean miss loss across rotations was 0.156 at nominal $\alpha=0.20$.

A fifth capture, recorded on a second operator after the pipeline was frozen, is the only test in this thesis that is blind to the alignment repair. On it the export row before the command already carries the target cell in 67\% of handovers against 5\% one row earlier, and the unlagged model is inflated 4.3-fold (AUPRC 0.512 against 0.119), so both methodological findings replicate. The predictive level transfers (gradient-boosted trees: AUPRC 0.137, 2.3x, AUROC 0.773), but the ranking of learners does not: logistic regression scores 0.261 (AUROC 0.842), and gradient-boosted trees do not beat the A3 proxy (0.159) there. The lead of the gradient-boosted trees is therefore a within-operator result. All numbers use a corrected measurement-report parser that recovers neighbour entries reporting RSRP without the optional RSRQ field.

Two properties of the deployed network are corrected against the earlier draft of this work. Weighted by handovers rather than by configurations, 75\% of A3-triggered handovers fire under a positive offset, so the operator's negative offsets belong to measurement configurations that rarely lead to mobility. The share of A3 reports left unmatched by a handover command within a 2~s attribution window is shown to have no unit-free value: 63\% of reports, 30\% of trigger episodes under a loose rule that lets overlapping episodes claim the same command, and 73\% once each command is matched to one episode. The separation between mobility and monitoring configurations survives all three and is widest under the strictest: 37\% unmatched for the dominant mobility profile against 95\% for the dominant monitoring profile. Unmatched is not the same as declined: the network's decisions are not observed. The contribution is a signalling-grounded, leakage-audited framework that converts measured handover events into coherent multi-horizon probabilities with a stated operating cost, together with a reproducible demonstration of how much of an apparently strong result on drive-test data can be timestamp alignment.

\vspace{1em}\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} LTE, handover prediction, Event A3, RRC signalling, discrete-time survival analysis, data leakage, evaluation protocol, conformal risk control, calibration, drive test.
