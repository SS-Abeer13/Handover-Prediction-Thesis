# Review — Reviewer "alfa"

## Summary

The thesis asks whether LTE handovers can be forecast a few seconds ahead from handset-side 1 Hz telemetry, and answers, unusually carefully, "barely, and here is exactly why you shouldn't trust the naive version of this result." Its three load-bearing claims are: (1) a one-row timestamp misalignment in the drive-test export inflates one-second AUPRC from a genuine 0.179 to a spurious 0.600, and this defect is mechanistically explained and replicated on two external corpora; (2) the choice of cross-validation unit matters more than the choice of model, with random-row splitting inflating AUPRC by 48% and reordering the leaderboard; and (3) a discrete-time hazard formulation buys guaranteed horizon-coherence "for free," though the thesis itself concedes it doesn't out-rank a two-line monotone projection. The self-critical apparatus here is genuinely unusual for a capstone thesis and largely does the reviewer's job in advance — negative results are reported, an early draft's flattering number is retracted in the same document that produced it, and four dimensions of validity are worked through explicitly. That said, "the paper polices itself hard" is not the same as "the paper is clean," and pressed on the one axis that matters most for a leakage story like this — whether the corrected experiment actually distinguishes 'real signal' from 'residual, un-audited leakage' — the thesis's own Table 5.1 contains a number it never interprets: the post-fix row (t−1) is still 15.6% contaminated overall versus 11.7% on isolated handovers, a gap concentrated in exactly the bursty regime that drives the headline AUPRC, and this gap is never tested the way the original 76% contamination was tested. My overall stance is major revision: the methodological contribution (the audit itself, as a technique) stands, but the headline predictive number the audit is supposed to have cleaned up has not itself been cleared of the artifact under adversarial scrutiny.

## Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The "mechanism confirmed" framing in the Contributions section outruns the statistical power of the test that supports it. **[Evidence — statistical rigour]**
   **Where** — Section 1.6, item 2 ("An account of why that happens, tested against the two predictions it makes") vs. Section 5.1's actual test (0/15 straddling handovers, 95% Wilson CI [0.0%, 20.4%]).
   **Why it matters** — A reader skimming the contributions list comes away with "the end-of-second aggregation account is confirmed." The actual evidence is a 15-event sample whose own confidence interval permits up to one-in-five contamination by chance — i.e., the data cannot statistically distinguish "the account is exactly right" from "the account is right about 80% of the time and something else explains the rest." The refinement invoked for the residual 211 cases (a "1-row exporter refresh delay") is validated only by re-examining the same dataset that produced the anomaly (comparing median margins of contaminated vs. residual cases, 0.522 s vs 0.477 s — not itself a strong separation), not by an independent prediction the way the primary account was tested against NUWiNS.
   **What would address it** — Either collect more straddling-boundary events (across further campaigns or the NUWiNS corpus, where boundary-crossing cases could be identified directly) to tighten the CI, or soften the contributions-list language to match the hedging already present in Section 5.1's body text.

2. **Issue** — The claimed novelty of applying conformal risk control to handover forecasting rests on an unaudited, self-described literature search, in a thesis that otherwise sets a much higher evidentiary bar for exactly this kind of claim. **[Novelty]**
   **Where** — Section 2.6 ("a systematic literature search was conducted... no published or preprint study prior to this work evaluated conformal prediction or conformal risk control for real-time handover event forecasting") vs. Section 2.3's protocol audit (108 screened, 22 audited, tabulated in Appendix D).
   **Why it matters** — The prediction-literature novelty claim in Section 2.3 is falsifiable by a reader: the 22 models are named, sourced, and gradeable. The conformal-novelty claim in Section 2.6 gives search strings and a date range but no count of what was screened or excluded, so a reader cannot check it and has to take the authors' word for an absence claim — precisely the kind of claim this thesis is elsewhere unusually insistent on not taking on faith (e.g., Section 1.2's own audit of the prediction literature's unchecked assumptions).
   **What would address it** — Apply the same screening discipline used in Section 2.3: report how many hits the conformal+mobility query returned and why each was excluded, in an appendix analogous to Appendix D.

3. **Issue** — The single explicit baseline (the "implemented A3 signal-margin proxy") is conceded by the authors to be a weakened stand-in for the real trigger, and no attempt is made to build a stronger one from data the thesis already has. **[Evidence — baselines]**
   **Where** — Section 4.7 ("this represents an implemented proxy rather than the complete operator handover controller") and Table 5.5 (A3 rule AUPRC 0.116 vs. LightGBM 0.179).
   **Why it matters** — The comparison "the learned hazard model beats the A3 rule by 2.6x vs 1.7x" is one of the few places in the thesis that reads like a headline result rather than a hedge, but it compares a tuned, feature-rich learner against a rule that, by the authors' own admission, cannot see the state (the TTT timer) that actually gates the real trigger. A persistence-based emulation of the TTT window — e.g., "margin has been positive for at least k of the last n samples," using exactly the decoded signalling timestamps the history block already exploits — is a cheap, obvious stronger baseline that was not tried.
   **What would address it** — Implement a TTT-window-emulating rule using the recovered per-configuration TTT values (Table 3.1) and report it alongside the instantaneous-margin proxy.

4. **Issue** — The pre-registration claim that makes the fourth (highway) campaign a genuine held-out test is committed only to an internal, non-public version-control repository. **[Reproducibility]**
   **Where** — Section 3.2 ("The freeze was committed to the project's internal version control repository prior to the Campaign 4 drive... not registered with an external independent trial registry, so it is auditable in git provenance but not independently certified").
   **Why it matters** — This is honestly flagged rather than hidden, which is to the authors' credit, but it is still unfalsifiable by any reader today: nothing external dates the freeze. Combined with "code released... committed on acceptance" (Table 2.1), every quantitative claim that depends on Campaign 4 being truly out-of-sample currently rests on trust in an unreleased commit log.
   **What would address it** — Release the manifest (or at minimum its commit hash and timestamp) now rather than "on acceptance," or register the freeze with a timestamped external service (even a public git tag pushed before the drive would suffice).

5. **Issue** — No discussion anywhere addresses instrumentation/device generalisability, despite the thesis's central finding being that a specific tool's export behaviour is an artefact-generating mechanism. **[Limitations]**
   **Where** — Absent from Section 5.13 (Threats to Validity) and Section 7.2 (Limitations); all four campaigns use "a commercial drive-test handset running Accuver XCAL" (Section 3.1), one model, one firmware/tool version throughout.
   **Why it matters** — Section 5.1 concludes "the alignment error is a property of the aggregation rule, not of a vendor or a network," supported by testing two *other* datasets collected with the *same* tool (XCAL) plus one collected with a different tool (G-NetTrack). That is evidence about the export *rule*, not about whether a second XCAL-equipped handset, chipset, or firmware version on the *same* operator would reproduce the same 76% contamination rate. Given how consequential a single-row export-timing quirk turned out to be, the absence of any second-device replication is the most glaring omission in an otherwise unusually thorough validity section.
   **What would address it** — State this explicitly as a limitation (it currently isn't), and if a second handset unit is available even briefly, replicate Table 5.1 on it.

6. **Issue** — The corrected ("post-audit") feature row is not itself shown to be clean, and the specific residual contamination visible in the paper's own numbers concentrates in exactly the regime that drives the headline result. **[Evidence — distinguishing test]**
   **Where** — Table 5.1: row t−1 (the row used for prediction after the one-row lag fix) is contaminated in 15.6% of all handovers vs. 11.7% of isolated handovers (>8 s from the previous command) — a ~4-point gap specific to non-isolated (bursty) handovers.
   **Why it matters** — The entire audit in Section 5.1 is built and validated around one contrast: row t+0 (badly contaminated, ~76-93%) vs. everything the fix is supposed to buy. But the fix's own output row (t−1) is not asserted to be at background/chance level, and the paper never checks whether that gap is itself leakage. This matters specifically because Section 5.8 reports that the model retains a 1.9x AUPRC lift on in-burst rows (Table 5.13) — the same regime where t−1 contamination is elevated — and because bursts (median inter-handover gap 3.7 s) are common enough in this corpus to materially affect the pooled 2.6x lift. The thesis performs exactly the right kind of distinguishing test for the *original* 76% leak (the 0/15 margin-crossing check, replicated on NUWiNS with a start-of-second vs. end-of-second manipulation) but never re-runs that same logic — margin-crossing tests, or an isolated-vs-burst contamination comparison specifically at t−1 — on the row the actual reported AUPRC of 0.179 is computed from. As it stands, "0.179" has not been distinguished from "0.179, partly still inflated by attenuated end-of-second leakage concentrated in bursts," which is the one alternative hypothesis this thesis is best positioned of any paper I've reviewed to have ruled out, and didn't.
   **What would address it** — Recompute Table 5.4's headline AUPRC restricted to isolated handovers only (>8 s from the previous command, where t−1 contamination is close to background), and compare it to the pooled figure; if it drops further, part of the 0.179 is leakage, not signal. Alternatively, extend the margin/δ diagnostic of Figure 5.1 to row t−1 directly rather than only to the raw row t+0.

## Minor concerns

- The problem-statement bullets in Section 1.3 contain unrendered HTML entities ("h1 &lt; h2 &lt; … &lt; hK"), a markdown-conversion artifact that needs cleaning before any journal submission.
- Table 5.9's asterisk system ("100%*" alongside a footnote explaining only 25% of rotations are feasible at α=0.05) is confusing on first read; the em-dash convention for infeasible cells is clearer and should be used consistently instead.
- The event-count reconciliation (957 → 938 → 934–937, explained across Sections 1.2, 3.4, and Appendix A.2) is correct but scattered; a single summary table early in Chapter 3 would save the reader from re-deriving it each time a new subset count appears.
- Repeated narration of "an earlier draft of this work claimed X, which is now withdrawn" (used well over a dozen times across chapters) suits a thesis defense narrative but would read as unusually self-referential in a journal manuscript; a Q1 target should consolidate this into a single limitations/erratum note rather than re-litigating the revision history chapter by chapter.
- Chapter 6 (Outcome-Based Education demonstration) and the front matter (declaration, approval, acknowledgements) are thesis-format artifacts; the text notes Chapter 6 is "omitted when compiled in journal mode," implying a dual-build system, but the version reviewed here mixes both — worth confirming the journal build strips these before submission.
- Declaration of competing interests (Section 6.6) is appropriately disclosed (supervisor co-authors the external validation dataset and the RL comparator), but it appears only in the OBE chapter, which will be stripped for journal submission — this disclosure needs to migrate to wherever the paper's actual limitations/COI section lands.
- Units are inconsistently given for "false alarms per hour" vs. "false alarms per kilometre" in the same paragraphs (Section 3.6 vs. Table A.2) without always stating which is primary; pick one as canonical and report the other parenthetically.

## Belief update

Two real, if modest, updates. First, on the object-level question — can 1 Hz handset telemetry provide useful multi-second handover early warning — this paper is consistent with (and sharpens) a prior that the answer is "not with this instrumentation": AUPRC lifts of 1.8–2.6x over a low prevalence floor, at false-alarm rates the authors themselves call undeployable, is a genuinely negative result reported as one, which is rare enough to be worth something on its own. Second, and more usefully, this paper raises my estimate of how often "impressive" drive-test ML results in this literature are contaminated by export/timestamp semantics rather than genuine signal — a 0.600→0.179 AUPRC collapse from a one-row lag is a striking, well-triangulated demonstration (two independent corpora, a directional manipulation on one of them) that this class of error is easy to introduce and easy to miss. I have not updated toward trusting the specific 0.179 figure as fully clean, for the reason in Major Concern 6 — the paper's own numbers leave that open. I have not updated at all on conformal risk control for cellular mobility being a validated tool; the paper is explicit that it isn't delivering an operating guarantee, and I take it at its word.

## Verdict

**Major revision.** The audit methodology and the intellectual honesty of the negative results are worth publishing, but the headline predictive number the audit is meant to have cleaned up is not itself shown to be free of the same leakage mechanism — Table 5.1's own 15.6%-vs-11.7% residual contamination gap at the corrected row, concentrated in the bursty regime that drives much of the reported lift, needs to be tested with the same rigor the paper applied to the original 76% leak before the 0.179 AUPRC figure can be taken as clean.
