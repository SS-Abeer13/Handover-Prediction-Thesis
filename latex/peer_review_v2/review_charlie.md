### Summary

This bachelor's thesis asks whether an LTE handover command can be forecast ahead of time from handset-reported measurements, and with what statistical guarantee, using four drive-test campaigns (4 sessions, 10,260 one-hertz samples, 957 signalling-decoded handover commands) on a single commercial operator in Dhaka/Gazipur. The authors first discover and quantify a severe timestamp-alignment artifact in their own one-hertz export (a row nominally "before" a handover already carries the post-handover serving cell 76% of the time), show that correcting it collapses one-second AUPRC from an initially reported 0.600 to 0.179, and then report the corrected number as the headline result: gradient-boosted trees under a discrete-time hazard formulation reach pooled AUPRC 0.179 (vs. a 6.8% prevalence floor, 2.6× lift, AUROC 0.777) at a one-second horizon under leave-one-campaign-out (LOCO) evaluation, rising to AUPRC 0.475 at five seconds. The paper further shows that weaker evaluation protocols (random-row or random-block splitting) inflate AUPRC by 13–48%, that a discrete-time survival/hazard parameterization removes horizon-ordering violations that plague independent per-horizon classifiers, and that conformal risk control can bound row-level miss rates within a campaign but cannot be certified across campaigns (exchangeability fails) or at all for the per-handover estimand at the 1 s horizon (35.8% of commands have no eligible prediction row). The paper's own framing is that its primary contribution is a leakage-audited measurement/evaluation framework rather than a deployable predictor.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The single "held-out" campaign used to support the generalization objective (O4) is not protected by the paper's own pre-registration, because the largest and most consequential methodological change (the alignment/lag audit) was performed retrospectively using data from all four campaigns, including the nominally held-out one.
   **Where** — Section 3.2 ("the freeze was committed... prior to the Campaign 4 drive," but "the alignment audit... was performed retrospectively after all four campaigns had been acquired"); Section 5.1; Section 5.6.
   **Why it matters** — The freeze covers "the feature set, the formulation, the model family, the hyperparameter search space and the evaluation protocol," but explicitly excludes the one change that swings one-second AUPRC by more than 3× (0.600 → 0.179). Because that fix was derived by inspecting contamination patterns across all four sessions — the "held-out" campaign's data necessarily contributed to discovering and validating the aggregation-rule hypothesis before the uniform one-row lag was applied to all four campaigns, including campaign 4 — campaign 4 cannot be described as a genuine, methodologically untouched out-of-sample test. The pipeline used to score the "generalization" campaign was itself calibrated in part on that campaign's own contamination pattern. This is leakage into protocol design, not leakage into model fitting, and it sits entirely outside the LOCO fold structure that the paper otherwise defends carefully.
   **What would address it** — Either collect a fifth campaign after the alignment-audit pipeline was frozen and report it as the true generalization test, or explicitly downgrade every claim resting on campaign 4 ("generalises... to a corridor and speed regime held out entirely," Objective O4) from a confirmatory pre-registered result to an exploratory, non-registered observation.

2. **Issue** — The one data point offered as evidence for generalization across "corridor and speed regime" (Objective O4) confounds those two factors completely, and is also a single uncontrolled observation confounding numerous additional variables (cell density, antenna tilt, time of day, traffic).
   **Where** — Section 3.2 ("the fourth campaign... is the only campaign that differs in both corridor and speed regime"); Sections 5.6, 5.10 (speed vs. carrier-relationship confound in ping-pong), 5.13.2 (acknowledged).
   **Why it matters** — Objective O4 promises to test generalization "to a corridor and speed regime held out entirely," implying two axes of variation can be assessed, but a one-campaign, two-simultaneously-varying-factor design cannot attribute the observed AUPRC drop (0.132 vs. 0.196–0.233 elsewhere) to speed, corridor, or any of the many uncontrolled covariates that differ between an urban loop and a highway. The paper is honest that this cannot be resolved ("the honest reading of the spread is that a fifth campaign could plausibly land anywhere inside it"), yet the abstract and objectives still present this as a generalization test that was "met," which overstates what a single confounded observation can show.
   **What would address it** — Reframe O4's highway result as an anecdote about one additional session rather than a generalization test, or design a controlled follow-up (same route at varied speed, or matched-speed different routes) that can actually separate the factors, as the authors themselves propose for future work regarding time-to-trigger (Section 7.3) but do not apply here.

3. **Issue** — Every cross-campaign comparison rests on n=4 independent units (sign-test power capped at p≥0.125 per the authors' own admission), yet summary language in the Abstract, Objectives, and Conclusions repeatedly uses confidence-implying phrasing ("the result generalises," "reduces ECE across all folds," "4 of 4... favouring hazard") without attaching that caveat at first mention.
   **Where** — Abstract; Section 5.4 (Table 5.8, "4 of 4" / "3 of 4" language); Section 5.13.4 (caveat stated, but decoupled from the claims it should qualify).
   **Why it matters** — A "k of 4" count has almost no discriminating power at this sample size; the same count is plausible under a fairly wide range of true effects, including null ones. Presenting it prominently in tables and prose without the n=4 caveat attached in the same breath risks readers (and downstream citers) treating within-campaign "consistency" as if it were statistically established rather than a small, non-random anecdote.
   **What would address it** — Attach the n=4/p≥0.125 caveat to every instance of "X of 4 campaigns" language rather than confining it to the Statistical Conclusion Validity subsection at the end of the chapter.

4. **Issue** — The abstract and Objective O3 frame the conformal risk-control results as a positive, numerically quotable finding ("the pooled empirical mean miss loss... was 0.153 at target α = 0.20"), when the paper's own analysis shows no certified guarantee exists anywhere across campaigns, and the operator-relevant per-handover estimand is provably inexpressible at the primary 1 s horizon.
   **Where** — Abstract; Sections 4.10, 5.5, 5.12 (O3 discussion: "the estimand an operator cares about is not expressible at all at the shortest horizon").
   **Why it matters** — A reader encountering the abstract's numeric claim before the qualifying material risks concluding the paper delivers a usable distribution-free bound, when the honest characterization — stated later — is that this is a wholly negative result dressed as a "diagnostic." Leading with a number that is later revealed not to be a valid guarantee is a claim-ordering problem that affects how the contribution will be cited.
   **What would address it** — State in the abstract's very first mention of conformal risk control that (a) no cross-campaign guarantee is certified due to violated exchangeability and (b) the per-handover bound is mathematically inexpressible at 1 s under the stated blanking policy, before giving any numeric miss-rate figure.

5. **Issue** — No code or data is available for independent verification at review time; release is deferred to "on acceptance."
   **Where** — Section 7.3 ("Release of code and data"); Appendix C.
   **Why it matters** — The thesis's most consequential claim (the end-of-second aggregation artifact, and its cross-instrument replication on the NUWiNS/Irish corpora) is a subtle pipeline-level discovery of exactly the kind that is easy to get wrong in implementation (e.g., off-by-one errors in "lag by one row," inconsistent handover-alignment definitions across the three corpora compared in Table A.3). None of this can be checked from prose and tables alone, and the raw vendor logs are stated to be non-redistributable even after acceptance.
   **What would address it** — Provide the alignment-audit script and derived row-level frames to reviewers now (even under a restricted-access arrangement), rather than only after acceptance.

6. **Issue** — The "independently collected" dataset and comparator method used for external validation (Sections 5.7, 5.11) are not independent: the thesis supervisor is a declared co-author of both, produced in the same department with the same instrument.
   **Where** — Section 6.6 ("Declaration of competing interests"); Section 5.7.
   **Why it matters** — The Abstract and Objective O4 both describe this as validation on "an independently collected public dataset," but the disclosed conflict (same supervisor, same department, same city/operator, same XCAL instrument) means this is closer to a same-lab replication than an independent external test — and even that replication could only be run on 6 of the model's 112 features because the public release lacks synchronous radio/GPS data (a limitation the authors state clearly, Section 5.7). Combined with Concern 2, this leaves the paper with essentially no evidence of generalization beyond a single confounded internal campaign and a same-lab, feature-impoverished cross-check.
   **What would address it** — Replace "independent" with an accurate label ("same-institution replication with overlapping authorship") throughout the abstract, objectives, and conclusions, and seek validation on a genuinely external dataset (different institution, ideally different operator) before claiming external generalization.

7. **Issue** — A large number of evaluation-protocol parameters (180 s block length, 60 s purge, 10 s burst cutoff, 2 s blanking window, the choice of exactly five horizons) were selected after the data were collected, and the reported "sensitivity analysis" (Table A.5) only perturbs each parameter locally by a factor of two rather than testing materially different designs.
   **Where** — Table A.5; Section 5.13.1 ("Post-hoc parameter thresholds... selected during protocol design").
   **Why it matters** — Local robustness around an already-chosen operating point cannot rule out that the specific values reported were implicitly favorable choices among many plausible alternatives explored during development (the paper documents extensive iteration between "earlier drafts" and the final version throughout Chapter 5). The claim that results are "robust" is therefore narrower than it sounds — it is robustness to small perturbations of a data-informed choice, not evidence the choice itself was unbiased.
   **What would address it** — State explicitly that these thresholds were chosen using the training campaigns before evaluation, and characterize Table A.5 as local sensitivity rather than as evidence against forking-path bias in the original selection.

### Minor concerns

- The manuscript is saturated with meta-commentary on its own revision history ("an earlier draft of this work claimed...", "this claim is withdrawn") — transparent and unusual, but a submitted paper should generally integrate corrected conclusions rather than narrate the correction process; as written it is hard to tell what is being claimed now versus what is being retracted.
- Table 5.9's footnote packs three distinct feasibility-floor computations (exchangeable pooling, per-campaign floors, rotation-level feasibility) into a single dense paragraph that is difficult to parse against the table it annotates.
- Acronym list (front matter) omits terms used later in the text, e.g. "PAV" (pool-adjacent-violators), "OBE," "KPI" usage inconsistencies.
- Several comparator citations relied on for the novelty/gap argument (e.g., Kabeer et al., Mandapati, Qchohi et al.) are recent, unreviewed preprints explicitly marked "not part of the screened set of Section 2.3," meaning the central "gap" claim leans partly on citations whose methodological details cannot be cross-checked against the paper's own audit criteria.
- The literature screening underlying Table 2.1 (108 screened, 22 audited) is not documented with the same rigor as the systematic search reported in Section 2.6 (which lists exact search strings, databases, and date ranges for the conformal-prediction novelty claim); the inconsistency in documented rigor between two similarly load-bearing literature claims should be resolved by giving the 108/22 screen the same specificity.
- Chapter 6 (Outcome-Based Education) and the declaration/approval front matter are appropriate for a BSc thesis but have no place in a journal manuscript; the text notes it is "omitted when compiled in journal mode," but as submitted for this review it adds substantial non-research content that a journal reviewer should not need to read through.
- Table 3.1's percentage breakdown and Table 5.14's per-profile conversion rates require cross-referencing multiple non-adjacent tables and appendix material to fully verify the "75% positive-offset" and "63%/30%/73%" headline percentages; a compact reconciliation table would help.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the paper is unusually candid about its own limitations and about a serious leakage artifact it discovered and corrected, but the study's only claims to external generalization rest on a single, retrospectively-contaminated, factor-confounded campaign and a same-lab "independent" dataset with an undisclosed-until-late conflict of interest, and its headline risk-control result is framed more positively in the abstract than the body's own analysis supports.
