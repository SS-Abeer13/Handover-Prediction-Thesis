# Review (Reviewer: delta)

### Summary
This EEE bachelor's thesis asks whether the next LTE handover can be forecast from handset-observable measurements, at multiple horizons, with a stated statistical guarantee. Using four drive-test campaigns (957 signalling-confirmed handovers, 10,260 one-hertz samples) on a single commercial LTE operator, the authors first uncover and correct a severe timestamp-alignment defect in their own 1 Hz export (a row stamped before a handover command already reflects the post-handover serving cell in 76% of cases), which collapses their one-second AUPRC from 0.600 to 0.179 once corrected. On the corrected data, they pose multi-horizon prediction as discrete-time survival (hazard-based, guaranteeing monotonicity across horizons) and report LightGBM as the best of seven learners (AUPRC 0.179 at 1s / 0.475 at 5s against prevalence floors of 6.8%/26.1%), compare four data-splitting protocols (showing random-row splitting inflates AUPRC by 48% relative to leave-one-campaign-out), and apply conformal risk control to bound miss rates, while explicitly flagging that the cross-campaign exchangeability assumption needed for the bound to be a real guarantee is not established. The headline contribution is framed as a leakage-audited measurement/evaluation framework rather than a deployable predictor.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The central conformal risk-control result is calibrated and evaluated on only 4 independent units (campaigns), which is far below the sample size needed for any of the paper's own feasibility-floor arithmetic to mean what it claims, and the "83% of 12 rotations" statistic conflates dependent rotations as if they added information.
   **Where** — Section 4.10 (Equation 4.12), Section 5.5, Table 5.9, and the abstract's claim "empirical holdout loss falling below α in 83% of 12 individual rotations."
   **Why it matters** — With only 4 campaigns, a leave-one-campaign-out conformal calibration has at most 4 truly distinct held-out evaluations; the "12 individual rotations" figure is not defined anywhere precisely enough to know whether it double-counts the same 4 campaigns under different (α, threshold) settings or genuinely draws 12 exchangeable calibration/test splits. If it is the former (which the surrounding text strongly suggests, since block counts are given as n ∈ {8,14,15,20} per campaign), then "83%" is a descriptive tally over highly dependent outcomes, not evidence of coverage frequency in any statistical sense — it cannot be interpreted as an empirical false-coverage rate the way the abstract's phrasing invites a reader to do. The paper is careful in prose to say "results are descriptive," but the number is still presented prominently (abstract, Section 5.5, Table 5.9, Chapter 7) in a way that borrows the rhetorical weight of a validation statistic. A statistically literate reader will ask: descriptive of what population, and with what standard error? None is given, and none can be meaningfully given with n=4.
   **What would address it** — Either drop the "83% of 12 rotations" framing from the abstract/conclusion (keep it, if anywhere, buried in Section 5.5 with an explicit statement that it has no inferential content), or replace it with the actual per-campaign realized losses (4 numbers) with no aggregate percentage at all, since that is the only quantity for which independence is defended elsewhere in the same thesis (Section 5.4's use of "4 of 4 campaigns" as the only defensible comparison unit). The inconsistency — treating 4 units as the only legitimate unit for AUPRC comparisons in Section 5.4, but then computing an aggregate "83% of 12" statistic for the conformal result in Section 5.5 — is itself a methodological contradiction that should be resolved.

2. **Issue** — No correction for multiple comparisons or selective reporting is applied across the very large number of comparisons the thesis runs (7 learners × 5 horizons × 4 splitting protocols × several calibration arms × several ablations × several ping-pong definitional grids), and while the authors are commendably explicit that "no multiplicity correction is applied" (Section 4.7), the justification given ("with four independent units none would be informative") does not follow — the multiplicity problem here is not about the 4 campaign units, it is about the dozens of researcher-degree-of-freedom comparisons (splitting windows, blank windows, burst cutoffs, feature-block ablations) that are each reported as a single point estimate.
   **Where** — Section 4.7 ("Nomultiplicitycorrectionisapplied..."), Table A.5 (sensitivity of four post-hoc windows), Section 5.8 (burst/quiet cutoff), Section 5.9 (three counting units for A3 conversion), Section 5.10 (nine-cell definitional grid for ping-pong).
   **Why it matters** — The paper's own Table A.5 shows that varying the purge window alone swings the blocked-vs-LOCO gap from -5.6% to +3.7%, and it explicitly says this "straddles zero" and is "not established... in either direction." That is an honest admission, but it also means several other single-point sensitivity results reported without a corresponding interval (e.g., the burst/quiet cutoff ordering, the quiet-lift figures in Section 5.8, the per-profile A3 conversion rates in Table 5.14) are subject to the same researcher-degrees-of-freedom risk without the same caveat attached. The thesis is inconsistent about which post-hoc choices get a sensitivity analysis and which do not.
   **What would address it** — Extend the Table A.5-style sensitivity sweep to every post-hoc threshold used in a headline claim (the burst/quiet split, the A3 report-conversion window, the ping-pong return window grid already exists but is not accompanied by an uncertainty statement on top of the definitional grid itself), and state explicitly, next to each headline number, whether its robustness to the underlying choice has been checked.

3. **Issue** — The paper reports point estimates for calibration error (ECE) and single "confidence intervals" from a block bootstrap that the paper itself has already shown (Section 5.3) understates variance because blocks are not independent, yet it continues to use exactly this bootstrap for the primary AUPRC intervals in Table 5.4 and Table 5.5, only switching to the (correct, but very coarse) 4-campaign spread for the paired hazard-vs-control comparison in Section 5.4.
   **Where** — Table 5.4, Table 5.5 (block-bootstrap CIs) versus Section 5.4/Table 5.8 (where the block-bootstrap interval is explicitly withdrawn as "manufactur[ing] a confidence interval narrower than the evidence supports").
   **Why it matters** — This is a direct internal inconsistency: the authors prove in one section that the block-bootstrap CI is invalid because blocks are correlated (Section 5.3's finding that block-splitting itself inflates AUPRC by 13%), then use that same invalid interval-generation method for the primary performance table (Table 5.4) that a reader is most likely to cite (e.g., "AUPRC 0.179, 95% CI [0.159, 0.204]"). If the block bootstrap is not trustworthy for testing a hazard-vs-control difference, it is not trustworthy for characterizing uncertainty in the headline AUPRC either — the same non-independence applies to both.
   **What would address it** — Either relabel every block-bootstrap interval in the thesis (not just Table 5.8) as "within-campaign, non-inferential spread" consistently, or drop the false appearance of precision in Table 5.4/5.5 and report only the 4-campaign range there too, matching the standard the authors apply to themselves in Section 5.4.

4. **Issue** — The nested nested hyperparameter search (20 trials × 7 learners × 4 folds × 3 seeds) is run on a dataset with roughly 600 positive rows at the 1-second horizon, and while the authors note this is "a large search relative to roughly six hundred positive rows," they do not quantify the risk that the search itself, even though it cannot see the held-out campaign, can still overfit to the *idiosyncrasies of the training-campaign mixture* in a way that a 4-fold rotation cannot detect, because each "outer fold" reuses the same 3 remaining campaigns for both search and final fit.
   <br>**Where** — Section 4.7 ("Every arm receives the same tuning budget..."), Section 5.2.
   **Why it matters** — With only 3 campaigns available for training+tuning in each outer fold, the 20-trial random search selects hyperparameters using the same 3 sessions it then trains the final model on (unless a further internal split is used, which is not described precisely — the text says the search is "scored on a calibration block held out of that fold's training data," which is a within-training-campaigns split, not a fourth independent unit). This means hyperparameter selection is optimizing against exactly the same 3-campaign idiosyncrasies that the final model is fit on, so held-out AUPRC could still be inflated relative to what a genuinely new corridor would show, even though "no held-out campaign is ever scored during a search" is technically true.
   **What would address it** — State explicitly how many independent units back the internal tuning split (it sounds like it is a temporal/row-level split within the 3 training campaigns, which reintroduces exactly the near-duplicate-row risk that Section 5.3 spends a whole section warning about), and, if so, flag this as a residual, not fully eliminated, leakage risk in Section 5.13's threats-to-validity list (it currently is not mentioned there).

### Minor concerns

- Table 2.1 and the surrounding audit narrative round-trip through overlapping claims ("Reports calibration... 0/22" appears identically twice at the end of Section 2.3, verbatim, suggesting a copy-paste duplication artifact in the manuscript).
- The abstract states "an empirical hypothesis falsified by none of the 15 handovers here whose execution crosses the boundary" — "falsified by none" is an odd double negative for "confirmed by all 15"; given n=15, this should be stated with appropriate humility about sample size rather than as if it strongly corroborates the mechanism.
- Reference [15] (Amirova et al.) and [39] (the RL comparator) both carry bracketed reproducibility notes ("Full author list to be completed," "Venue to be confirmed") that should not remain in a document being reviewed as final — these read as unresolved TODOs in the reference list.
- Table 5.9's row for α=0.05 uses an asterisked "0.000*" / "100%*" combination that is explained only in a dense footnote; this table would benefit from plain-language annotation given how much of the thesis's core "boundary result" contribution rests on it.
- The n for cross-campaign calibration units is reported inconsistently as "12 individual rotations" (abstract, Section 5.5) versus "n ∈ {8,14,15,20}" (four values, Table 5.9) without a clear one-line explanation of how four campaigns produce twelve rotations; this should be spelled out explicitly the first time either number appears.
- Minor arithmetic self-consistency point: Table 3.2 lists 957 total handover commands, but the abstract and multiple in-text passages alternate between "938" (quality-controlled) and "957" (raw decoded) without always flagging which is in use in a given sentence (e.g., Figure 3.1's caption "N=938" versus Section 1.2's "957 signalling-confirmed handover commands").

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the underlying leakage/alignment audit is a genuinely valuable and unusually honest methodological contribution, but the statistical treatment of uncertainty is internally inconsistent (a bootstrap the authors themselves discredit is still used for the headline table), and the conformal risk-control result's "83% of 12 rotations" statistic is presented with more inferential weight in the abstract than 4 independent campaigns can support.
