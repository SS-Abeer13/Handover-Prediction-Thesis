### Summary
This thesis asks whether the next LTE handover can be forecast ahead of the reactive Event A3 trigger, using four drive-test campaigns (4 continuous sessions, 10,260 one-hertz samples, 957 RRC-signalling-confirmed handover commands) on a single commercial operator. Its most consequential finding is methodological rather than predictive: a timestamp-alignment audit shows that in 76% of handovers the one-hertz export row stamped just before the command already reflects the post-handover serving cell, so an uncorrected model's apparent one-second AUPRC of 0.600 collapses to 0.179 once every export feature is lagged by one row. Under a discrete-time hazard formulation evaluated by leave-one-campaign-out cross-validation, gradient-boosted trees reach AUPRC 0.179 (2.6× lift, AUROC 0.777) at one second and 0.475 (1.8×) at five seconds, modestly ahead of an implemented A3 signal-margin proxy (AUPRC 0.116). The paper also reports a distribution-free (conformal) risk bound that is descriptive rather than certified across campaigns, corrects two prior characterisations of the deployed A3 configuration, and documents several negative results, explicitly framing its contribution as the leakage-audited measurement framework rather than a deployable predictor.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The single most consequential analytic decision (the one-row lag and which feature columns to lag) was derived by inspecting contamination evidence pooled across *all four* campaigns, including the campaign that the leave-one-campaign-out protocol treats as "held out," so the generalization claim is not a blind test of the finished pipeline.
   **Where** — Section 3.2 ("the alignment audit of Section 5.1 … was performed after all four campaigns, so no campaign is out of sample with respect to it"); Section 5.1 (the audit pools evidence across the whole corpus to establish the end-of-second mechanism and the one-row rule); Objective O4 and Section 5.6.
   **Why it matters** — The paper's rhetorical structure treats Campaign 4 as an experimental control ("driven after the modelling decisions… had been recorded," "the only campaign that differs in both corridor and speed regime, so it is reported as a held-out campaign throughout"). But the feature-construction rule actually applied to Campaign 4 during evaluation (lag every export column) was chosen using contamination statistics that included Campaign 4 itself. This is a design leak at the level of pipeline construction, not row-level leakage — exactly the "leakage in training examples" category the paper itself invokes (via Kaufman et al.) to describe the original defect it found. A skeptic should ask: would the audit have concluded "lag by exactly one row" if only Campaigns 1–3 had been available? The paper does not show this, so the reported LOCO numbers for Campaign 4 cannot be cleanly attributed to genuine generalization.
   **What would address it** — Re-derive the alignment/lag rule using only Campaigns 1–3, freeze it, and then apply it unmodified to Campaign 4 as a genuinely blind test; report whether the audit's conclusions and the resulting AUPRC/AUROC figures for Campaign 4 change.

2. **Issue** — The "freeze" of modelling decisions before Campaign 4 was collected is attested only by the authors' own private version history, not by any independently verifiable timestamp.
   **Where** — Section 3.2: "The freeze was recorded in the project's own version history between the 3rd and 4th Campaigns in 2026, not registered with a third party, so it is auditable but not independently timestamped."
   **Why it matters** — The credibility of every "held-out campaign" claim in the paper rests on trusting that the feature set, model family, and evaluation protocol genuinely did not change after seeing Campaign 4. Given how many numbers in this same thesis are explicitly flagged as revisions of an "earlier draft" that got things wrong (the report-conversion rate, the offset-weighting claim, the compounding-error argument, the ping-pong lever claim, the omission of monotone-projection controls), the base rate of analytic revision in this project is high. Without third-party registration, a reviewer cannot rule out that the "freeze" itself was adjusted retrospectively along with everything else.
   **What would address it** — Use an external, timestamped registration mechanism (e.g., a signed commit hash lodged with the supervisor or an OSF pre-registration) for any future confirmatory claim of this kind.

3. **Issue** — The claim of generalisation "across corridors and speed regimes" (Objective O4) is drawn from a single campaign in which corridor, speed, carrier mix, cell density, and time-of-day all covary, so no factor can be attributed the observed change in performance.
   **Where** — Section 3.2 (Campaign 4 "differs in both corridor and speed regime"); Section 5.10 explicitly concedes the same confound for the ping-pong analysis ("with one campaign per regime, speed is completely confounded with route, cell density, carrier mix and time of day"); Objective O4 in Section 1.4.
   **Why it matters** — A held-out session that differs simultaneously along every axis is a test of "does the model transfer to this one other bundle of conditions," not a test of speed-regime generalisation specifically. The paper's own Section 5.10 admits this for ping-pong but does not carry the same caution into the framing of O4's "success," where AUROC 0.765–0.805 across four held-out campaigns is presented as evidence the result "generalises... to a corridor and speed regime held out entirely."
   **What would address it** — A minimal 2×2 design (same corridor at two speeds; two corridors at matched speed) before any claim naming speed regime specifically as a validated axis of generalisation.

4. **Issue** — The cross-campaign conformal risk-control result reports "12 individual rotations" as if they were 12 independent trials, when they are pseudo-replicated draws from only 4 independent campaigns.
   **Where** — Section 5.5 and Table 5.9: "empirical holdout loss falling below α in 83% of 12 individual rotations."
   **Why it matters** — With 4 campaigns, calibrate-on-one/test-on-another rotations number at most 12, but each campaign contributes to multiple rotations (as calibration set in some, test set in others), so the 12 outcomes are correlated through shared campaign identity. A single unusually favourable or unfavourable campaign can dominate several of the 12 "successes/failures," making 83% look more robust than 4 independent data points can support — precisely the kind of statistical over-counting the paper is careful to avoid everywhere else (e.g., its explicit refusal to run a sign test with n=4).
   **What would address it** — Report the 12 outcomes grouped by which campaign served as the test set, and state plainly that the effective sample size for this claim is 4, consistent with the paper's own standard elsewhere.

5. **Issue** — Two of the three external-validity checks (the "independent" public dataset and the RL comparator) are not independent of the authors' own research lineage, and this is only partly reflected in how the results are framed.
   **Where** — Section 5.7 ("collected by a different group of people... using the same operator, the same city, and the same XCAL licence, in the same department of the same institution"); Section 6.6 Declaration of Competing Interests (supervisor is co-author of both the external dataset and the RL comparator method).
   **Why it matters** — The transfer experiment in Section 5.7 is described as testing "collection, route and time — not operator, and not radio access technology," which is honest, but the paper still uses the word "independent collection" in headline text (Section 1.6 uses similarly qualified language, but Table 5.11's caption and surrounding prose lean on "independently collected"). Combined with the RL baseline being reimplemented in-house from a method description of a paper from the same lab, rather than run with the original authors' code, there is a nontrivial risk that both the "external" test and the "weak baseline" result are shaped by shared assumptions/instrumentation rather than being a genuinely arm's-length check.
   **What would address it** — Seek a dataset and comparator implementation with no institutional overlap, and, failing that, ask the original RL-comparator authors to validate the reimplementation.

6. **Issue** — The causal account of *why* the alignment defect occurs (end-of-second aggregation, ~50–75 ms commit window) is validated only on external corpora the authors themselves downsampled, never on a native-rate re-export of the actual campaigns being predicted on.
   **Where** — Section 5.1: "it does not establish that XCAL's own one-hertz exporter aggregates that way, which remains inferred from its consequences and which only a native-rate re-export of these captures would settle; Section 5.13 carries that as a residual risk."
   **Why it matters** — The one-row lag "fix" applied throughout Chapter 5 is a plausible, evidence-consistent correction, but it is not a directly confirmed one for this dataset. If the true per-session commit latency varies (e.g., with cell load, device firmware, or across the "two academic terms" separating the campaigns), a single fixed one-row lag could over- or under-correct in ways the paper cannot detect without the native-rate ground truth it says it lacks. Since this correction is what turns the headline AUPRC from an inflated 0.600 into 0.179, its own evidentiary status deserves the same scrutiny the paper otherwise applies to the original leak.
   **What would address it** — Obtain even one native-rate (sub-second) re-export of a single campaign to directly confirm the write-timing rule on the actual instrument, rather than relying on inference from a different tool (G-NetTrack) and a downsampled third-party corpus (NUWiNS).

7. **Issue** — A load-bearing scope figure is stated but never reconciled with later numbers describing the same underlying limitation.
   **Where** — Section 1.5: "the measurement export is sampled at one hertz, which caps the fraction of handover events that can in principle be resolved at 90.5%," versus Section 3.6/4.10/5.5, which state the eligible-prediction-window ceiling as 64.2% at one second (35.8–36.4% unresolvable).
   **Why it matters** — These two numbers describe closely related concepts (how much of the event set the 1 Hz grid can, in principle, resolve) but differ by more than 25 percentage points with no visible arithmetic connecting them anywhere in the text. Given the number of quantities in this thesis that were revised between drafts, an unreconciled figure like this is exactly where a stale or superseded computation would hide, and it undercuts confidence in the "everything is regenerated from stage outputs" reproducibility claim of Appendix C.
   **What would address it** — Define the population and denominator behind the 90.5% figure explicitly and show how it relates to (or supersedes) the 64.2%/36.4% figures, or remove it.

### Minor concerns
- Table 5.2 reports the "lagged" one-second AUPRC as 0.179 ("this work"), while Table 5.3 reports the ostensibly identical alignment condition ("every export column (v2, as reported throughout)") as 0.160; the difference (tuned vs. fixed hyperparameters) is explained elsewhere but not cross-referenced at the point of the numbers, inviting a reader to compare the wrong cells.
- The reference list contains multiple unresolved editorial placeholders — "[Author list to be completed from the ACM record before submission]" (ref. 6), "[Full author list to be completed from the MDPI record before submission]" (ref. 15), "[confirm the version DOI before submission]" (ref. 38), "[Venue to be confirmed; a ResearchGate record exists]" (ref. 39) — indicating the manuscript is not yet in submission-ready form.
- Ref. [39], an unpublished, unrefereed in-house manuscript, is used as the paper's nearest comparator baseline; its methodological fidelity in reimplementation cannot be checked by a reader.
- Chapter 6 ("Demonstration of Outcome Based Education") and its associated appendices are capstone/accreditation material with no place in a journal submission and would need to be removed entirely before Q1 submission.
- Event counts drift slightly across tables without a pointer at first use (957 vs. 938 vs. 934–937 in Table A.2 per horizon); the 957→938 reconciliation is explained in Section 3.4, but the further per-horizon variation in Table A.2 is not explained inline.
- "Distribution-free risk control," "conformal risk control," and "conformal prediction" are used close to interchangeably in places; a reader unfamiliar with Angelopoulos et al. would benefit from one clear statement distinguishing the mean-risk guarantee actually used from coverage-style conformal prediction.
- The extracted thesis text shows heavy word-merging/typesetting artifacts in the front matter (likely a PDF-extraction issue rather than a defect of the original document), which should be verified against the source PDF before any of the above observations are treated as authorial errors rather than extraction noise.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the leakage-audit contribution and the honesty about negative results are genuinely valuable, but the held-out campaign is not actually blind to the pipeline's defining preprocessing decision, key generalisation and risk-control claims rest on confounded or pseudo-replicated evidence from only four independent units, the causal mechanism behind the central correction is unconfirmed on the paper's own instrument, and the manuscript still contains submission-readiness defects (placeholder citations, an accreditation chapter) inconsistent with a Q1 journal target.
