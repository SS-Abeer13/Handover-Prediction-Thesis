### Summary
The manuscript investigates whether upcoming cellular handovers in a commercial LTE network can be reliably anticipated up to five seconds in advance from terminal-side measurements, and evaluates the methodological validity of existing predictive approaches. Across four continuous drive-test campaigns in Dhaka and Gazipur totalling 10,260 samples and 957 RRC-decoded handover commands, the authors uncover a severe timestamp alignment artifact in standard 1 Hz diagnostic exports (XCAL) where 75.9% of rows stamped immediately prior to a handover already contain the post-handover serving cell identity. By lagging features by one sample to enforce strict causality, the authors show that 1-second look-ahead AUPRC drops drastically from an illusory 0.600 to 0.179 (2.6x lift over a 6.8% prevalence floor; AUROC 0.777), and that naive random-row splitting inflates performance by 48% due to high temporal autocorrelation. To prevent horizon-incoherent predictions ($F_1 > F_2$), the authors formulate multi-horizon forecasting as a discrete-time hazard model, and they benchmark distribution-free conformal risk control on operational miss rates. The core result is that while a modest advance warning signal exists, published machine-learning gains on drive-test data are largely artifacts of unstated timestamp alignment rules and invalid data-splitting protocols.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The reported 95% confidence intervals and paired difference tests rely on a block bootstrap of 180-second blocks within campaigns, which violates the independence assumption of the bootstrap, artificially suppresses between-campaign variance, and overstates statistical precision.
   - **Where** — Section 4.9 (lines 222–224); Table 5.3 (lines 115–131); Table 5.6b (lines 241–268); Section 5.13 (lines 676–678).
   - **Why it matters** — *Steelman:* The authors demonstrate commendable scientific integrity by explicitly rejecting random-row splitting and reporting a ladder of four splitting protocols, proving that 180-second blocks within the same session share trajectory, base stations, and radio environments. *Press:* Having established in Section 3.4 and Section 5.3 that 180-second blocks are not independent and that random block-splitting inflates AUPRC by 13%, the authors nevertheless resample these exact same 180-second blocks to construct their 95% confidence intervals and paired difference tests. Resampling dependent sub-blocks within continuous sessions destroys the continuity of the time series at block seams while treating pseudo-replicated segments as exchangeable units. Consequently, the reported bootstrap intervals (e.g., Table 5.3: AUPRC 1 s [0.159, 0.204]) are substantially narrower than the true empirical spread across the four independent campaigns (Table 5.8: AUPRC ranges from 0.132 on Highway to 0.233 on Urban Loop). More critically, in Table 5.6b, the authors declare that the hazard model achieves a statistically significant calibration advantage over independent heads based on an interval of `[-0.015, -0.006]` excluding zero. Because the resampled units are spatially and temporally autocorrelated, this interval is anti-conservative, giving a false sense of confirmatory statistical significance for differences that fall well within the between-campaign variance.
   - **What would address it** — Replace or augment the within-campaign block bootstrap with a leave-one-campaign-out jackknife or cluster-robust variance estimator that treats the four campaigns as the primary sampling units. Where $N=4$ clusters prevent formal inference, the authors should remove claims that paired confidence intervals "exclude zero" in Table 5.6b, presenting the differences as descriptive per-campaign spreads rather than statistically confirmed advantages.

2. **Issue** — The study performs dozens of simultaneous hypothesis tests and parameter comparisons across learners, horizons, and stratifications without family-wise error rate control or false discovery rate adjustment.
   - **Where** — Section 4.7 (lines 191–195); Table 5.4; Table 5.6b; Section 5.8 (Table 5.11).
   - **Why it matters** — In Section 4.7, the authors note that *"No multiplicity correction is applied, because with four independent units none would be informative; the reader is given the full table and the intervals instead"*. Yet, throughout Chapter 5, selective significance claims are made. Table 5.6b evaluates 10 separate paired comparisons against control arms across AUPRC and ECE, highlighting five instances where the "Interval excludes zero". Across 7 learners, 5 horizons, multiple feature subsets, and post-hoc subgroup splits (such as quiet vs. burst rows in Table 5.11), conducting multiple unadjusted tests across a small dataset creates a substantial risk of false discovery (Type I error inflation), particularly when comparing models whose performance gaps (e.g., LightGBM vs. MLP vs. TCN at 0.179 vs. 0.173 vs. 0.167) are marginal.
   - **What would address it** — Apply Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) or Holm-Bonferroni corrections across the family of paired comparisons in Table 5.6b. Furthermore, explicitly state in the prose that subgroup comparisons (e.g., quiet vs. burst rows) are exploratory post-hoc analyses rather than pre-registered confirmatory findings.

3. **Issue** — The effective sample size of the study ($N=4$ independent sessions comprising 2.9 hours of driving) provides inadequate statistical power to support definitive conclusions regarding the inferiority of deep sequence architectures or domain adaptation.
   - **Where** — Section 3.6 (lines 173–177); Section 4.7 (lines 189–195); Section 5.2 (Table 5.4); Section 5.11 (Table 5.15).
   - **Why it matters** — *Steelman:* The authors correctly argue that their four continuous sessions represent genuine independent mobility units, avoiding the rampant pseudo-replication common in the literature. *Press:* However, with only 4 sessions and ~938 modeling events, the study is severely underpowered to generalize broad model architecture rankings. The authors conclude that deep sequence models (GRU, TCN, Transformer) and unsupervised domain adaptation (Deep CORAL) offer no advantage over gradient-boosted trees. While this holds for this specific, small tabular dataset, recent sequence-based deep learning and continual learning frameworks for cellular handover (e.g., arXiv:2510.02958 and arXiv:2512.11841) show that temporal architectures require substantial data diversity to learn generalizable representations. Concluding that sequence modeling is inherently unsuited to handover forecasting conflates the limitations of a 2.9-hour dataset with the inductive biases of the model families.
   - **What would address it** — Recalibrate claims in Sections 5.2, 5.11, and 7.1 to explicitly qualify that the poor performance of sequence models and domain adaptation is conditional on the limited volume and geographic diversity of this 4-session corpus, rather than a universal property of cellular mobility forecasting.

4. **Issue** — The conformal risk control procedure fails under cross-campaign distribution shift, but the paper neither models the covariate shift nor provides a shift-adjusted valid coverage mechanism.
   - **Where** — Section 4.10 (lines 235–256); Section 5.5 (lines 300–327, Table 5.7).
   - **Why it matters** — Conformal risk control guarantees $E[R_{n+1}(\hat{\lambda})] \le \alpha$ strictly under exchangeability. The authors demonstrate that when calibrated on one campaign and tested on another, the bound is violated in up to 33% of rotations (e.g., at $\alpha=0.30$, realized loss is 0.261 with only 67% of rotations respecting the bound). While reporting this failure is honest, leaving it uncorrected leaves an operator with no actionable guarantee. Recent advances in distribution-free wireless inference under distribution shift and confounding (e.g., Qchohi et al., arXiv:2609.05073) utilize likelihood ratio weighting or interventional guardrails to preserve coverage guarantees despite covariate shift. Without quantifying the Wasserstein or total variation distance between campaigns or applying weighted conformal risk control, the operational utility of the conformal framework remains theoretical.
   - **What would address it** — Incorporate an analysis of covariate shift between campaigns (e.g., tracking speed and RSRP drift) and discuss or implement weighted conformal risk control, or explicitly state in the abstract and conclusion that the distribution-free guarantee is empirically invalidated by inter-campaign corridor shifts.

5. **Issue** — Stratifying performance post-hoc into "in burst" versus "quiet" rows introduces survivor and selection bias while confounding prevalence shifts with predictive lift.
   - **Where** — Section 5.8 (lines 435–456, Table 5.11); Figure 5.13.
   - **Why it matters** — In Table 5.11, rows are partitioned into those occurring 2–10 s after a prior handover ("in burst", prevalence 11.2%) and those with no handover in the previous 10 s ("quiet", prevalence 4.3%). The authors claim the model earns its primary advantage on quiet rows (3.1x lift vs. 1.9x in bursts). However, every burst of handovers is initiated by an initial handover that necessarily occurs on a "quiet" row. The manuscript does not distinguish between a model predicting an isolated handover and one predicting the first event of a rapid cluster. Moreover, comparing AUPRC lift across two subsets whose baseline prevalences differ by a factor of 2.6 is statistically misleading because the maximum achievable AUPRC and precision-recall curve geometry depend strongly on base prevalence.
   - **What would address it** — Evaluate whether "quiet" true positives represent isolated handovers or burst initiators, and report standardized metrics (such as calibrated Brier score or standardized partial AUPRC) across strata to prevent base-rate confounding.

6. **Issue** — There is a structural disconnect between the optimization objective (discrete-time survival log-likelihood) and the primary reported evaluation metric (AUPRC).
   - **Where** — Section 4.4 (lines 95–101); Section 4.5 (lines 127–138); Section 5.4 (lines 243–268).
   - **Why it matters** — The discrete-time survival formulation minimizes binary cross-entropy on expanded (sample, bin) pairs, optimizing proper scoring and calibrated hazard estimates. In contrast, the paper's headline metric and ranking criterion across chapters is AUPRC. The authors admit in Section 4.5 that rank metrics are invariant to monotonic probability distortions, and in Section 5.4 they acknowledge that the hazard formulation yields no ranking advantage over independent classifiers (-0.001 AUPRC difference). While the hazard model is defended primarily on coherence and calibration, the paper repeatedly defaults to AUPRC when declaring winners across architectures (Table 5.4) and protocols (Table 5.5).
   - **What would address it** — Rebalance the empirical narrative to present strictly proper scoring rules (such as Brier score and log-loss) alongside AUPRC as co-equal primary metrics throughout Chapter 5, aligning the evaluation directly with the hazard model's probabilistic objective.

### Verdict
**Major revision**. The manuscript provides an outstanding and devastating methodological audit of temporal leakage and split-induced inflation in drive-test learning, but substantial revisions are required to resolve the anti-conservative block-bootstrap variance estimation, apply rigorous multiple-comparison corrections to paired tests, appropriately bound the cross-campaign conformal risk breakdown, and fix mathematical notation errors.
