### Summary
This thesis investigates whether proactive handover forecasting is viable from handset-side LTE telemetry and, crucially, establishes the methodological prerequisites required for such predictions to be valid. Using 2.9 hours of continuous drive-test logging across four campaigns in Dhaka and Gazipur (10,260 1-Hz samples, 957 RRC-decoded handover commands), the authors uncover a severe timestamp alignment artifact: in 75.9% of handovers, the 1-Hz export row stamped before the command already contains post-handover serving cell metrics, which inflates uncorrected 1-second AUPRC from 0.179 to 0.600. Addressing this, the authors propose a discrete-time hazard formulation that guarantees monotonic cumulative incidence across five look-ahead horizons (0.5 to 5.0 s) from a single model fit, evaluate models under a strict leave-one-campaign-out (LOCO) protocol, and benchmark conformal risk control and Hawkes point processes. Under LOCO evaluation with lagged features, gradient-boosted trees achieve an AUPRC of 0.179 at 1 second against a 6.8% prevalence floor (2.6× lift, AUROC 0.777) compared to 0.116 for the deployed Event A3 baseline, though at a 5% false-positive operating point this detects only 34.0% of handovers with 179 false alarms per hour and a median lead time of 0.47 seconds.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue**: The manuscript relies on block-bootstrapping over 180-second blocks to construct confidence intervals and claim that paired differences exclude zero, despite previously demonstrating that these blocks are statistically dependent and invalid as evaluation units.
   - **Where**: Section 3.4 (lines 63–65), Section 4.9 (lines 213–225), Section 5.3 (Table 5.5), Section 5.4 (Table 5.6b, lines 241–250), and Section 5.13 (lines 675–678).
   - **Why it matters**: In Section 3.4 and Section 5.3, the authors establish that 180-second segments of a single continuous drive session share trajectory, radio environment, and cell sequences, showing that treating them as independent units inflates AUPRC by up to 13% to 48%. Yet, in Section 5.4 (Table 5.6b), the authors construct 95% confidence intervals for paired differences between models (e.g., asserting a statistically supported calibration advantage for the hazard model over independent heads: difference -0.010, 95% CI [-0.015, -0.006], "Interval excludes zero: yes") by resampling these exact 180-second blocks. This is classical pseudo-replication: resampling autocorrelated blocks within four sessions ignores session-level clustering, severely deflates standard errors, and yields artificially narrow confidence intervals. At the level of the four truly independent units (the campaigns), the two-sided sign-test has an absolute minimum p-value of 0.125, meaning that no statistically significant separation exists. Claiming that paired intervals "exclude zero" while acknowledging zero statistical power at the campaign level is an internal methodological contradiction.
   - **What would address it**: Remove claims that paired differences "exclude zero" based on the block bootstrap. Either report hierarchical or cluster-robust confidence intervals that respect campaign-level clustering, or report empirical per-campaign paired differences directly, explicitly stating that formal statistical hypothesis testing is unpowered with $N=4$ independent sessions.

2. **Issue**: The text states that the hazard formulation achieves a lower calibration error on zero of the four held-out campaigns, contradicting the reported pooled paired difference, while the supporting per-campaign calibration data promised for Appendix A are absent.
   - **Where**: Section 5.4 (lines 275–280, line 287), Table 5.6b, and Appendix A.
   - **Why it matters**: In Section 5.4 (line 279), the authors write: *"The hazard arm has the lower calibration error on 0 of the four held-out campaigns at one second; the exact two-sided sign-test p-value for four paired observations cannot fall below 0.125, so no significance is claimed and the per-campaign numbers are reported in full in Appendix A."* If the uncalibrated independent baseline exhibited lower (better) ECE on all four individual campaigns, then the pooled result in Table 5.6b (`Hazard - control: -0.010`, favoring Hazard) is either a statistical artifact of aggregation across campaigns with differing sample sizes and prevalences (Simpson's paradox) or a typographical error. If it is an aggregation artifact, pooling produces a misleading impression of superiority that holds in none of the individual sessions. Furthermore, Appendix A contains only Tables A.1, A.2, and A.3; the per-campaign calibration data promised to the reader are missing. In addition, line 287 cites an ECE of "0.024 at the one-second horizon", which directly contradicts both Table 5.3 (0.012) and Table 5.6 (0.036).
   - **What would address it**: Provide the complete per-campaign calibration breakdown (ECE and Brier score) in Appendix A. Clarify whether the hazard arm had lower ECE on 0 of 4 campaigns or if this was an inverted statement. If the hazard model was indeed worse on all four campaigns, explain the mechanism behind the pooled reversal and retract the claim that the hazard formulation improves calibration over unconstrained heads. Reconcile the discrepancy between 0.012, 0.036, and 0.024.

3. **Issue**: Extensive model selection and post-hoc pipeline tuning across 1,680 fits per protocol and multiple horizons are presented without family-wise error control or a pre-registered confirmatory hold-out.
   - **Where**: Section 3.2 (lines 29–30), Section 4.7 (lines 191–195), Section 5.2, and Table 5.4.
   - **Why it matters**: The experimental search space spans 20 trials across 7 learners, 4 folds, and 3 seeds (1,680 fits per protocol), evaluated across five horizons, multiple feature blocks, and four coherence controls. While nested cross-validation prevents test leakage during hyperparameter optimization, the authors acknowledge that it does not eliminate selection bias across model families. Reporting LightGBM as the leading model (AUPRC 0.179 [0.159, 0.204]) over MLP (0.173 [0.152, 0.202]) and TCN (0.167 [0.146, 0.197]) without multiple-comparison adjustment overinterprets differences that lie entirely within mutual confidence bounds. Moreover, Section 3.2 admits that the alignment audit and feature lagging—the most critical design choices—were conducted after all four campaigns were collected. The entire pipeline was thus iteratively refined with knowledge of the full corpus, meaning the reported metrics reflect exploratory optimization rather than confirmatory evaluation on an untouched split.
   - **What would address it**: Explicitly temper the narrative surrounding model rankings, stating that LightGBM, MLP, and TCN are statistically indistinguishable under the LOCO protocol. Formally state in the methodology that the post-audit findings represent exploratory model development, and discuss the associated researcher degrees of freedom.

4. **Issue**: The branching ratio of a rejected, statistically misspecified parametric Hawkes model is used as a numerical summary of handover event clustering.
   - **Where**: Section 4.11 (lines 269–277) and Section 5.10 (lines 615–620).
   - **Why it matters**: Section 5.10 reports that the Ogata time-rescaling residual test rejects the exponential-kernel Hawkes process on this data, yet the manuscript continues to report the branching ratio ($\alpha/\beta = 0.653$) as a quantitative summary of clustering. In point-process statistics, when the underlying parametric intensity function is formally rejected by residual diagnostics, maximum likelihood estimates of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are inconsistent, and the branching ratio loses its theoretical interpretation as offspring per event. Relying on an index from a rejected model to draw structural conclusions is methodologically unsound. Furthermore, the paper omits the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test statistic, degrees of freedom, and p-value, preventing independent evaluation of the degree of misspecification.
   - **What would address it**: Report the formal goodness-of-fit statistics (Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance and p-value) for the residual analysis. Replace the parametric branching ratio with standard non-parametric clustering metrics (such as the empirical auto-covariance of inter-arrival times or Ripley's K-function), or evaluate alternative kernels (e.g., power-law or sum-of-exponentials) that satisfy residual checks.

5. **Issue**: Conformal risk control is claimed as a primary contribution despite failing distribution-free coverage across campaigns and being mathematically inexpressible for event losses on 1-Hz data.
   - **Where**: Section 1.6 (Contribution 5), Section 4.10 (lines 230–264), and Section 5.5 (Table 5.7).
   - **Why it matters**: Contribution 5 and Objective O3 promise a distribution-free risk guarantee for handover warning. However, as Table 5.7 documents, under cross-campaign deployment (the realistic operational scenario), the empirical miss rate violates the nominal bound in 17% to 33% of rotations because drive-test sessions violate the fundamental exchangeability assumption. More critically, at the 1-second horizon, 36.4% of handover commands lack a valid sample row within their lead window due to 1-Hz sampling grid quantization, rendering finite-sample risk bounds for event-level loss completely inexpressible ($\alpha \le 0.20$ is feasible in 0% of rotations). While the authors deserve credit for honestly presenting these empirical failures in Section 5.5, framing "distribution-free risk control" as an accomplished contribution in the Abstract and Section 1.6 overstates what the framework delivers in practice.
   - **What would address it**: Reframe Contribution 5 and Objective O3 to explicitly characterize conformal risk control as a negative/boundary result: show that exchangeability fails under spatial-temporal distribution shifts in drive tests, and that temporal discretization imposes a hard mathematical floor on event-level risk guarantees.

6. **Issue**: The absolute performance gain over the native Event A3 baseline is marginal (+0.063 AUPRC at 1 second), resulting in an operational profile characterized by severe false-alarm rates and sub-second lead times.
   - **Where**: Section 5.2 (Table 5.3, Table 5.4), Table A.2, and Section 5.12.
   - **Why it matters**: Correcting for the alignment leak reduces the 1-second AUPRC from 0.600 to 0.179, leaving an incremental +0.063 gain over the deployed, parameter-free Event A3 baseline (0.116). When mapped to an operational operating point at a 5% false-positive rate (Table A.2), the model detects only 34.0% of handovers, triggers 179 false alarms per hour (one every 20 seconds), and provides a median lead time of just 0.47 seconds. In cellular networks, 0.47 seconds is barely sufficient to complete target-cell preparation over the X2 interface, while 179 false reservations per hour would impose unacceptable signaling and resource overhead. While the Abstract concedes that the predictor is not deployable, the introductory motivation continues to suggest that advance warning can facilitate proactive target preparation.
   - **What would address it**: Align the introductory motivation and conclusions with the empirical findings: state unambiguously that 1-Hz offline drive-test features provide insufficient discriminative power for proactive target preparation, and clarify that the primary value of the work is forensic and methodological rather than predictive.

### Verdict
**Major revision**. While the manuscript provides an exemplary, rigorously documented, and devastatingly honest audit of data leakage and timestamp misalignment in cellular telemetry, a major revision is necessary to resolve pseudo-replication in the bootstrap uncertainty estimates, reconcile contradictory calibration findings between pooled and per-campaign metrics, provide missing appendix data, fix equation labeling errors, and align the contribution claims with the empirical limits of conformal risk control.
