### Summary
The paper investigates whether LTE handovers can be forecast ahead of time from handset-observable measurements on a commercial network, framing the task under discrete-time survival analysis and auditing evaluation protocols. Using four continuous drive-test campaigns in Bangladesh (Dhaka and Gazipur, totalling 2.9 hours, 10,260 1-Hz samples, and 957 RRC-decoded handover commands), the authors report that tabular gradient-boosted trees achieve a 1-second AUPRC of 0.179 over a 6.8% prevalence floor (2.6× lift, AUROC 0.777) under leave-one-campaign-out evaluation. The core of the manuscript, however, is a methodological autopsy of its own data and prior literature: the authors demonstrate that an aggregation artefact in commercial XCAL exports leaked future serving-cell identities into 76% of rows stamped before a handover command, inflating uncorrected 1-second AUPRC from 0.179 to 0.600, while standard random-row splitting inflated performance by an additional 48%. Overall, this is an exceptionally candid, intellectually disciplined piece of engineering self-policing that dismantles the inflated claims prevalent in cellular ML (e.g., [2510.02958], [2512.11841]); nevertheless, its positive engineering contribution remains heavily circumscribed by an operationally undeployable operating frontier (179 false alarms/hour at 1-second horizon for a 34% detection rate), a sample size of only four independent campaigns, and a critical reliance on aggregate metrics and a single idealized trace rather than systematic, unsifted qualitative error analysis.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The manuscript frames the system as providing an advance warning suitable for proactive network resource management, but at the evaluated operating points the warning produces an unviable false-alarm burden with negligible usable lead time `[Narrative — overclaiming]`.
   - **Where** — Abstract (lines 7–8), Section 1.2 (lines 28–30), Section 5.2 (Table 5.3), Section 5.5 (Figure 5.2, Table A.2).
   - **Why it matters** — Section 1.2 claims that "a warning one second ahead of a handover command is sufficient time for the network to complete target-cell preparation." However, Figure 5.2 and Table A.2 reveal that at the 5% false-positive rate operating point, the model achieves a detection rate of only 34% at 1 second while generating 179 false-alarm episodes per hour—an alarm every 20 seconds of driving—with a median lead time of just 0.47 seconds. For target cell preparation in LTE/5G, where RRC signalling, X2/Xn transport, and admission control require several hundred milliseconds, 0.47 seconds is practically near zero margin, and an alarm triggering 179 times an hour for 34% coverage would severely degrade radio resource management rather than optimize it.
   - **What would address it** — Explicitly qualify the narrative in the Abstract and Section 1.2: state plainly that under causal, leakage-free evaluation, the offline predictor does not achieve an operating point viable for proactive target-cell preparation, and position the work strictly as an analytical diagnostic framework rather than an advance-warning system.

2. **Issue** — The empirical foundation rests on only four continuous driving sessions on a single operator, rendering all cross-campaign statistical tests uninformative and limiting the validity of the reported uncertainty bounds `[Evidence — statistical rigour]`.
   - **Where** — Section 3.2 (Table 3.2), Section 3.6 (lines 174–176), Section 4.9 (lines 222–224), Section 5.6 (Table 5.8).
   - **Why it matters** — While the authors commendably recognize that adjacent 180-second blocks are not independent and enforce leave-one-campaign-out (LOCO) as the only sound unit, having $N=4$ independent units means the minimum attainable two-sided sign-test p-value is 0.125. Furthermore, the block bootstrap intervals in Tables 5.3 and 5.4 are conditional on these four specific sessions and do not capture session-to-session or environmental variance. In Table 5.8, the 1-second AUPRC drops from 0.233 on the urban loop to 0.132 on the highway—a 43% drop. With only four points, an informed reader cannot determine whether this spread represents random variance across corridors, driver habits, or time of day.
   - **What would address it** — Report hierarchical or multi-level variance components explicitly acknowledging that between-campaign variance dominates within-campaign bootstrap intervals, and refrain from treating the pooled point estimate as a stable population parameter until evaluated across at least 8–10 independent sessions.

3. **Issue** — The discrete-time survival formulation is presented as a major structural innovation that guarantees horizon monotonicity, but the authors' own empirical controls show that trivial post-hoc projections achieve identical coherence and calibration without the survival machinery `[Novelty — formulation attribution and marginality]`.
   - **Where** — Section 1.6 (Contribution 4), Section 4.3 (lines 81–86), Section 5.4 (Table 5.6, Table 5.6b).
   - **Why it matters** — The survival framing (Equations 4.2–4.5) is mathematically elegant, but Table 5.6 demonstrates that a simple two-line cumulative-maximum projection (`independent + cumulative max`) or Pool Adjacent Violators (`independent + PAV`) on standard independent binary classifiers also reaches exactly 0.0% ordering violations, identical 1-second AUPRC (0.157 vs 0.158, paired difference -0.000, 95% CI [-0.012, 0.010]), and requires no held-out calibration split. Furthermore, as conceded in Section 4.1 and 5.4, the censoring-aware likelihood (Equation 4.6) is entirely inert because no row in the dataset is censored inside the 5-second horizon. Posing the hazard formulation as a foundational advantage over independent heads overclaims its actual empirical utility.
   - **What would address it** — Reframe Section 4.3 and Contribution 4 to explicitly acknowledge that the hazard formulation is a convenient computational packaging (fitting 1 model instead of $K$) rather than an empirical necessity, since standard monotonic projection trivially resolves coherence at zero metric cost.

4. **Issue** — The experiments do not differentiate whether the model is learning genuine radio-frequency propagation physics or merely memorizing handover burst dynamics and urban intersection kinematics `[Evidence — distinguishing tests]`.
   - **Where** — Section 3.2 (Figure 3.1), Section 5.8 (Table 5.10, Table 5.11, lines 457–463), Section 5.10 (Figure 5.17, 5.18).
   - **Why it matters** — Section 3.2 notes that handover events heavily cluster at specific road junctions (Figure 3.1), and Section 5.10 shows that handovers exhibit strong self-excitation (Hawkes branching ratio 0.653, median inter-command gap 3.7 s). In Table 5.11, a model using *only* the 6-feature history block reaches 1.7× lift in bursts. While the full model reaches 3.1× lift on quiet rows, Table 5.10 shows that the strongest single feature is `sig_s_since_a3` (time since last A3 report, AUROC 0.723) and `serving_sinr` (AUROC 0.723), while the actual Event A3 serving-to-neighbour gap achieves only 0.618 AUROC. This leaves open the plausible alternative hypothesis that the model is primarily acting as a non-linear timer of network control-plane cadence and junction slowing rather than forecasting RF propagation boundaries.
   - **What would address it** — Conduct a controlled ablation on synthetic or randomized trajectories where geographic and velocity features are scrubbed or permuted while preserving local RF profiles, directly testing whether the model retains predictive power when decoupled from intersection kinematics and burst history.

5. **Issue** — Critical experimental thresholds and protocol design decisions were selected post-hoc after observing initial leaky results, with the pre-campaign freeze documented only internally `[Evidence — post-hoc hypothesis shaping]`.
   - **Where** — Section 3.2 (lines 29–30), Section 3.4 (lines 63–65), Section 4.6 (lines 147–150), Section 4.9 (lines 220–222).
   - **Why it matters** — The authors disclose that the campaign freeze between campaigns 1–3 and campaign 4 was recorded in their internal git history rather than pre-registered, and that the alignment audit (lagging features by 1 row) was developed after all four campaigns were completed. Furthermore, the 180-second block length, the 60-second temporal purge, the 10-second "quiet" vs "in-burst" cutoff, and the 2-second post-handover blanking window are all post-hoc heuristics. In ML literature, post-hoc tuning of splitting and filtering thresholds on small datasets creates an undocumented degree of freedom that can inadvertently optimize test-set metrics.
   - **What would address it** — Provide sensitivity curves over the arbitrary parameters: demonstrate that the results do not abruptly collapse if the block size is varied from 120 s to 300 s, the purge from 30 s to 120 s, or the burst threshold from 5 s to 20 s.

6. **Issue** — The underlying raw diagnostic logs cannot be released due to proprietary vendor restrictions, limiting independent verification of the ASN.1 decoding timeline and the raw physical-layer export semantics `[Reproducibility — proprietary data barriers]`.
   - **Where** — Section 2.3 (Table 2.1), Section 7.3 (lines 54–56), Appendix C.
   - **Why it matters** — Table 2.1 criticizes the audited literature for lack of data release (only 1/22 releasing data). However, the authors themselves can only commit to releasing derived CSV frames and decoded event streams, not the raw XCAL diagnostic files (`.xdl`/`.xcap`). Since the core scientific discovery of the paper hinges on the exact timestamp semantics and ASN.1 decoding of `VarMeasConfig` across RRC reconfiguration messages, third-party researchers cannot independently audit whether the custom replay logic (Section 3.3.3) or the ~60 ms exporter latency estimation (Section 5.1) contain subtler decoding bugs.
   - **What would address it** — Provide a fully synthetic or anonymized raw binary packet capture snippet exhibiting the exact RRC configuration sequence and XCAL export records to allow independent testing of the end-to-end parser pipeline.

7. **Issue** — The manuscript relies on a single idealized RF trajectory to motivate the problem and an unverified qualitative assertion to explain the 22.5% alignment residual, while completely omitting qualitative trace-level case studies or error analyses of model failures `[Evidence — cherry-picked qualitative examples]`.
   - **Where** — Figure 1.1 (Section 1.1, lines 18–21), Section 5.1 (lines 84–86), Figure 3.1 (Section 3.2, lines 23–26), and Chapter 6 (lines 1–249).
   - **Why it matters** — This is the paper's primary methodological blind spot. First, in Figure 1.1, the authors motivate Event A3's reactive delay using a single, hand-picked pair of RF traces displaying textbook-clean, monotonic signal crossing. In real-world urban deployments, RF trajectories are plagued by fast fading, shadowing dips, and multipath scattering that repeatedly cross entry conditions without triggering handovers (as proven by the authors' own finding that 73% of A3 episodes are declined by the network). Showing only one pristine trace creates a misleading intuition of clean physical mechanics. Second, in Section 5.1, the authors explain away the fact that 22.5% of handovers with positive margin $m > 0$ do *not* contaminate row $t$ by asserting qualitatively: "That is the exporter refreshing the serving-cell field one row late, not a second mechanism." Not a single trace, timeline diagram, or individual example is provided to substantiate this claim, leaving it as an unproven post-hoc rationalization. Third, and most glaringly, Chapter 6 is titled `ch6_demonstrative_case_studies.txt` in the manuscript source, yet contains zero demonstrative case studies—it was repurposed entirely as an administrative Outcome-Based Education (OBE) checklist! Nowhere in Chapter 5 or 6 do the authors provide unsifted, qualitative time-series traces of: (a) a true-positive early warning, (b) an actual false-alarm episode (what RF/kinematic anomaly caused the trees to fire?), or (c) a false-negative missed handover. In applied ML (per Neel Nanda's guidance), aggregate metrics hide whether a model is learning genuine features or exploiting edge artifacts; inspecting unsifted qualitative examples is the only way to ground what the model is actually doing.
   - **What would address it** — Replace the administrative boilerplate of Chapter 6 (or add an extensive section in Chapter 5) with genuine qualitative case studies: display unsifted, randomly sampled time-series traces showing RSRP, RSRQ, speed, model probabilities, and signalling flags for True Positives, False Positives, and False Negatives; provide trace-level timeline evidence verifying the 22.5% delayed-refresh hypothesis; and plot the spatial handover distribution of the held-out highway campaign alongside the urban campaigns.

- **Re-establishment Metric Ambiguity**: In Table 3.2, Campaign 10 Sept reports 113 re-establishments, while Table 3.3 reports only 19 commands followed by a re-establishment within 5 s and 30 re-establishments within 2 s of a command. Provide a brief sentence clarifying when the remaining ~64 re-establishments occurred relative to session boundaries.

State explicitly in the surrounding text that $\delta$ and $x$ are normalized to seconds, as $x$ is described in the adjacent paragraph in milliseconds (p90 of 99 ms), which may momentarily confuse readers regarding unit consistency.
- **Unexplained Linear Model Dominance in ARM B**: In Table 5.5, under untuned leave-one-campaign-out evaluation, regularized logistic regression achieves AUPRC 0.167 while untuned LightGBM achieves 0.160. Add a brief sentence explaining why unconstrained linear decision boundaries generalize slightly better across campaigns in the default hyperparameter regime.
- **Missing Recent Literature Context**: The protocol audit in Section 2.3 accurately characterizes 22 models, but misses recent sequence-based handover studies on real/simulated traces such as Kabeer et al., 2025 (`[2510.02958]`), which benchmarks GRU/LSTM on drive-test traces without reporting split hygiene, and Mandapati, 2025 (`[2512.11841]`), which explores spatial zone hold-outs. Citing these modern benchmarks directly strengthens the audit's diagnostic scope.

### Belief update
- **Belief updated upward (+0.40 probability shift)**: Timestamp alignment leakage in aggregated tabular drive-test exports (specifically 1-Hz summaries that reflect the close of an interval) is a massive, silent confounder in mobile systems research. The demonstration that row $t$ summarizes $[t, t+1)$ and leaks future serving-cell assignments into 76% of pre-handover rows—accounting for 96% of an apparently stellar 0.600 AUPRC—is a brilliant, highly generalizable finding that completely invalidates naive benchmarks in the wireless ML literature.
- **Belief updated upward (+0.25 probability shift)**: Standard random-row cross-validation in cellular time series produces severe, architecture-dependent performance inflation (+48% for GBDT vs +7% for linear models), fundamentally distorting model comparison and leaderboard rankings.
- **Belief updated downward (-0.35 probability shift)**: Advance handover prediction from handset-side 1-Hz radio telemetry is practically viable for proactive target cell preparation. Under sound, leakage-free evaluation, predicting handovers 1 second ahead yields an AUPRC of only 0.179, and achieving a 34% recall costs 179 false alarms per hour with a median lead time of only 0.47 seconds—making proactive handover preparation virtually unworkable at 1 Hz without sub-second signalling hooks.
- **Belief unchanged (0.00 shift)**: Posing multi-horizon prediction as discrete-time survival analysis does not provide any empirical advantage over independent classifiers with simple monotonic post-processing; it is a convenience wrapper, not a predictive breakthrough.

### Verdict
**Major revision**: While the timestamp alignment audit is a triumph of scientific integrity that saves the paper from publishing a massive artifact, the manuscript requires a major revision to eliminate its reliance on cherry-picked qualitative examples—specifically by replacing the administrative OBE filler of Chapter 6 with unsifted, trace-level case studies of model successes and false alarms, providing physical trace evidence for the 22.5% delayed-refresh residual, and qualifying the narrative regarding practical deployability.
